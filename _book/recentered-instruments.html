16  Recentered Formula Instruments

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Many treatments are computed from a formula that combines a random shock with non-random exposure. Medicaid eligibility is a state’s rules applied to a person’s income. Import exposure is national industry shocks applied to a region’s industry mix. The Bartik instrument of the previous chapter is one such formula. This chapter covers the general version of Borusyak and Hull (Borusyak and Hull 2023), their result on which formula is best (Borusyak and Hull 2026), and what both mean for shift-share.

16.1 Formula instruments

Unit \(i\) has outcome \(y_i\) and treatment \(x_i\), with a constant effect \(\beta\):

\[ y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i . \tag{16.1}\]

The treatment is \(x_i = h_i(g, w, u)\), where \(h_i\) is a known function, \(g\) collects the random shocks, \(w\) collects predetermined variables such as income or industry shares, and \(u\) collects anything else. The identifying assumption puts all the randomness on the shocks (Borusyak and Hull 2026, Assumption 1):

\[ E[\varepsilon_i \mid g, w] = E[\varepsilon_i \mid w] . \tag{16.2}\]

The error may depend on \(w\) in any way. Poorer people may have worse outcomes, and regions with more manufacturing may have been declining anyway.

A formula instrument is \(z_i = f_i(g, w)\) for a fixed function \(f_i\). Its expected value over the shock draws, holding \(w\) fixed, is the expected instrument \(\mu_i = E[z_i \mid w]\). The recentered instrument \(\tilde z_i = z_i - \mu_i\) is valid, because by iterated expectations and Equation 16.2,

\[ E[\tilde z_i \varepsilon_i] = E\big[\tilde z_i\, E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]\big] = E\big[E[\tilde z_i \mid w]\, E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]\big] = 0 . \]

Without recentering the same steps give \(E[z_i \varepsilon_i] = E[\mu_i E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]]\), which is not zero when the expected instrument is correlated with the predictable part of the error. Subtracting \(\mu_i\) and controlling for \(\mu_i\) are equivalent by Frisch–Waugh–Lovell. Computing \(\mu_i\) requires the design: how the shocks could have been drawn. We redraw them from that design, or permute them if they are exchangeable, and average the formula over the draws.

Any recentered formula is valid, so we can pick the most precise. Borusyak and Hull (2026, Theorem 1) show it starts from the best prediction of the treatment, recentered:

\[ \tilde z = E[x \mid g, w] - E[x \mid w] . \tag{16.3}\]

The full optimal instrument adds two refinements, partly controlling for the predictable part of the error and GLS weighting, which need a model of the error. The authors drop them. Their algorithm predicts the treatment from its formula, recenters the prediction by redrawing the shocks, and uses the result as the instrument. When \(x_i\) depends only on \((g, w)\), the prediction is \(x_i\) itself.

16.2 Shift-share as a formula instrument

The Bartik instrument \(B_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k} g_k\) of Equation 15.2 is the linear formula, shares times shocks. It differs from Equation 16.3 in two ways.

The first is recentering. Suppose the industry shocks are exchangeable within period \(t\), with mean \(\bar g_t\), and let \(S_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k}\) be region \(\ell\)’s sum of shares. Then

\[ \mu_\ell = S_\ell\, \bar g_t . \tag{16.4}\]

If the shares sum to one, \(\mu_\ell = \bar g_t\) is common to all regions and period effects absorb it, so Bartik is already recentered. If they are incomplete, as with manufacturing shares, heavily manufacturing regions get a large instrument in every draw, and Bartik is biased unless we control for \(S_\ell\) interacted with period (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 2022).

The second is the formula. Take two regions with half their employment in electronics, and an import shock to electronics. Region A’s plants compete with Chinese imports. Region B’s make military components and do not. Bartik gives both half the shock, but only A’s import competition rises, so the instrument varies where the treatment does not. The best predictor gives A half the shock and B nothing, then recenters each by its own expected value. Both instruments are valid, and the second has the stronger first stage. When the treatment really is shares times shocks, the best predictor is Bartik itself and the two differ only by recentering. The formula matters when the treatment has more structure than Bartik uses: eligibility rules, export exposure, transport networks, nonlinear indices.

The formula instrument asks for more: the treatment’s formula and an explicit shock design. With heterogeneous effects, different instruments weight units differently, so a gain in precision is a gain for a different weighted average (Borusyak and Hull 2026, Proposition D1).

16.3 Simulation

The aim is to compare a shock-only instrument with the recentered treatment. There are 50 states and 10,000 people. Income \(\text{inc}_i\), in multiples of the poverty line, is log-normal with median 1.4. Each state draws an eligibility threshold \(T_k\) from \(\{0.5, 1, 1.38, 2\}\) with equal probabilities, and

\[ x_i = 1[\text{inc}_i < T_{s(i)}], \qquad y_i = 0.3\, x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i = -0.5 \log \text{inc}_i + \alpha_{s(i)} + \nu_i , \tag{16.5}\]

where \(s(i)\) is \(i\)’s state, \(\alpha_k \sim N(0, 0.3^2)\) is a state effect and \(\nu_i \sim N(0, 1)\). Poorer people are more often eligible and have higher errors, so OLS is biased. Expected eligibility \(\mu_i\) is the share of the four thresholds above \(\text{inc}_i\).

We compare OLS (the same as instrumenting \(x_i\) with itself); the simulated instrument of Currie and Gruber (1996), the share of all 10,000 people eligible under their state’s threshold; the recentered treatment \(x_i - \mu_i\); and OLS of \(y\) on \(x\) and \(\mu\), which is the controlled form of the recentered treatment (Borusyak and Hull 2026, footnote 8). Holding the people fixed, we redraw the 50 thresholds 1,000 times, so only the shocks change. The people and draws are stored as CSVs so the Julia companion computes the same numbers.

Code
people <- read_csv("data/recenter_people.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
draws  <- as.matrix(read_csv("data/recenter_policy_draws.csv",
                             show_col_types = FALSE)[, -1])
pool <- c(0.5, 1.0, 1.38, 2.0)
beta_true <- 0.3
mu    <- sapply(people$income, function(v) mean(v < pool))  # expected eligibility
gener <- sapply(pool, function(t) mean(people$income < t))  # state generosity

coef_x <- function(f) {
  nm <- intersect(c("fit_x", "x"), names(coef(f)))[1]
  c(est = unname(coef(f)[nm]), se = unname(se(f)[nm]))
}

one_draw <- function(r) {
  thr <- draws[r, people$state]
  d <- mutate(people, x = as.numeric(income < thr), y = beta_true * x + eps,
              z_sim = gener[match(thr, pool)], z_rc = x - mu, mu = mu)
  fits <- list(feols(y ~ x, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z_sim, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z_rc, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ x + mu, d, cluster = ~state))
  rbind(sapply(fits, coef_x),
        r2 = c(NA, cor(d$x, d$z_sim)^2, cor(d$x, d$z_rc)^2, NA))
}

mc <- simplify2array(mclapply(seq_len(nrow(draws)), one_draw, mc.cores = 6))

tibble(estimator = c("OLS", "Simulated instrument", "Recentered treatment",
                     "OLS controlling for mu"),
       mean     = apply(mc["est", , ], 1, mean),
       sd       = apply(mc["est", , ], 1, sd),
       coverage = apply(abs(mc["est", , ] - beta_true) <= 1.96 * mc["se", , ], 1, mean),
       first_stage_R2 = apply(mc["r2", , ], 1, mean)) |>
  kable(digits = 3)
estimator mean sd coverage first_stage_R2
OLS 0.637 0.049 0.000 NA
Simulated instrument 0.302 0.167 0.937 0.263
Recentered treatment 0.300 0.086 0.927 0.589
OLS controlling for mu 0.300 0.080 0.935 NA

OLS averages 0.637 against a true 0.3. The three other estimators are centred on 0.3, with coverage of 92.7% to 93.7% for clustered 95% intervals with 50 clusters. The simulated instrument has a standard deviation of 0.167 across draws, the recentered treatment 0.086, and its controlled form 0.080. The simulated instrument varies only across states, so its first-stage \(R^2\) is 0.26. The recentered treatment varies within states, among people whose income lies between the lowest and highest thresholds, and its \(R^2\) is 0.59.

16.4 The China shock

The ADH regression has incomplete shares: lagged employment shares of 397 manufacturing industries in each of 722 commuting zones, over two periods. ADH control for the start-of-period manufacturing share with one coefficient for both periods. Under exchangeable shocks within period, Equation 16.4 says the right control is \(S_\ell\) times the period’s mean shock, which is 4.8 in the first period and 17.3 in the second. A stricter design treats shocks as exchangeable only within the 20 two-digit SIC sectors in each period, giving \(\mu_\ell = \sum_m S_{\ell m} \bar g_{mt}\), where \(S_{\ell m}\) is the zone’s share in sector \(m\) and \(\bar g_{mt}\) the sector’s mean shock. All fits use ADH’s controls, census-division effects, population weights and standard errors clustered by state, with the GPSS replication files.

Code
adh <- read_csv("data/adh_master.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
tr  <- read_csv("data/adh_shares_triplets.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
shk <- read_csv("data/adh_shocks.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)

by_row <- function(v) {                        # sum over industries, by zone
  out <- numeric(nrow(adh)); s <- rowsum(v, tr$row)
  out[as.integer(rownames(s))] <- s[, 1]; out
}
sector  <- paste(shk$sic87dd %/% 100, shk$year)
g_bar_m <- tapply(shk$g, sector, mean)

adh <- mutate(adh, B = by_row(tr$share * shk$g[tr$col]), S = by_row(tr$share),
              mu2 = by_row(tr$share * g_bar_m[sector[tr$col]]))

controls <- "l_sh_popedu_c + l_sh_popfborn + l_sh_empl_f + l_sh_routine33 +
             l_task_outsource + t2 | division"
iv <- function(extra) {
  feols(as.formula(paste("d_sh_empl_mfg ~", extra, controls, "| shock ~ B")),
        adh, weights = ~weights, cluster = ~statefip)
}
fits <- list("ADH controls"                     = iv("l_shind_manuf_cbp +"),
             "Without the manufacturing share"  = iv(""),
             "Design 1: S x period"             = iv("S + S:t2 +"),
             "Design 2: sector expected instrument" = iv("mu2 +"))
tibble(specification = names(fits),
       estimate = sapply(fits, \(f) coef(f)["fit_shock"]),
       se       = sapply(fits, \(f) se(f)["fit_shock"])) |>
  kable(digits = 3)
specification estimate se
ADH controls -0.596 0.100
Without the manufacturing share -0.712 0.083
Design 1: S x period -0.267 0.087
Design 2: sector expected instrument -0.144 0.117

The first row reproduces ADH’s −0.596: an increase in import exposure of $1,000 per worker over a decade lowers the manufacturing employment share by about 0.6 percentage points. Controlling for the expected instrument period by period gives −0.267 (0.087). ADH’s control is close to \(S_\ell\), with a correlation of 0.92, but one coefficient cannot absorb a term whose slope more than triples between periods. The sector design gives −0.144 (0.117), because it no longer uses the fact that electronics and toys received larger shocks than food or paper.

Each row is right under its own design, and the data cannot tell us which design holds. Recentering forces the design to be stated, and here it matters a great deal.

16.5 The Medicaid application

Borusyak and Hull (2026) instrument Medicaid eligibility in the 2014 ACA expansion with actual minus expected eligibility, treating expansion as random within the governor’s party. The first-stage partial \(R^2\) rises from 0.01 to 0.80 over the simulated instrument and the standard error on crowd-out falls by 44%. Most of the gain comes from the 18% of the sample whose eligibility the expansion could change; outside it, the recentered instrument is zero, and in the full sample without an exposure control it is less precise than the simulated one (Borusyak and Hull 2026, Table A4). Their 2012–2013 placebo on private insurance is −0.011, half the main estimate of −0.023 (Table A2).

16.6 Summary

Shift-share is one formula instrument, with the formula and the design fixed by convention. Any formula of shocks and exposure is valid once its expected value over the shock design is subtracted, and the most efficient formula is the recentered best prediction of the treatment. For Bartik this gives two checks: whether incomplete shares require controlling for the sum of shares times the mean shock, and whether the treatment depends on the shocks through more than shares times shocks. In the ADH data the first check alone moves the estimate from −0.60 to −0.27.

For the shift-share construction, the share and shock views, and inference, see the previous chapter, Shift-Share Instrumental Variables.

period gives −0.267 (0.087). ADH's control is close to $S_\ell$, with a
correlation of 0.92, but one coefficient cannot absorb a term whose slope more
than triples between periods. The sector design gives −0.144 (0.117), because
it no longer uses the fact that electronics and toys received larger shocks
than food or paper.

Each row is right under its own design, and the data cannot tell us which
design holds. Recentering forces the design to be stated, and here it matters
a great deal.

## The Medicaid application

@borusyak-hull-2026 instrument Medicaid eligibility in the 2014 ACA expansion
with actual minus expected eligibility, treating expansion as random within
the governor's party. The first-stage partial $R^2$ rises from 0.01 to 0.80
over the simulated instrument and the standard error on crowd-out falls by
44%. Most of the gain comes from the 18% of the sample whose eligibility the
expansion could change; outside it, the recentered instrument is zero, and in
the full sample without an exposure control it is less precise than the
simulated one [@borusyak-hull-2026, Table A4]. Their 2012–2013 placebo on
private insurance is −0.011, half the main estimate of −0.023 (Table A2).

## Summary

Shift-share is one formula instrument, with the formula and the design fixed
by convention. Any formula of shocks and exposure is valid once its expected
value over the shock design is subtracted, and the most efficient formula is
the recentered best prediction of the treatment. For Bartik this gives two
checks: whether incomplete shares require controlling for the sum of shares
times the mean shock, and whether the treatment depends on the shocks through
more than shares times shocks. In the ADH data the first check alone moves the
estimate from −0.60 to −0.27.

For the shift-share construction, the share and shock views, and inference,
see the previous chapter, [Shift-Share Instrumental Variables](shift-share-iv.qmd).
