16  Recentered Formula Instruments

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Many treatments are computed from a formula that combines a random shock with non-random exposure. Medicaid eligibility is a state’s rules applied to a person’s income. Import exposure is national industry shocks applied to a region’s industry mix. The Bartik instrument of the previous chapter is one such formula. This chapter covers the general version of Borusyak and Hull (Borusyak and Hull 2023), their result on which formula is best (Borusyak and Hull 2026), and what both mean for shift-share.

16.1 Formula instruments

Unit \(i\) has outcome \(y_i\) and treatment \(x_i\), with a constant effect \(\beta\):

\[ y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i . \tag{16.1}\]

The treatment is \(x_i = h_i(g, w, u)\), where \(h_i\) is a known function, \(g\) collects the random shocks, \(w\) collects predetermined variables such as income or industry shares, and \(u\) collects anything else. The identifying assumption puts all the randomness on the shocks (Borusyak and Hull 2026, Assumption 1):

\[ E[\varepsilon_i \mid g, w] = E[\varepsilon_i \mid w] . \tag{16.2}\]

The error may depend on \(w\) in any way. Poorer people may have worse outcomes, and regions with more manufacturing may have been declining anyway.

A formula instrument is \(z_i = f_i(g, w)\) for a fixed function \(f_i\). Its expected value over the shock draws, holding \(w\) fixed, is the expected instrument \(\mu_i = E[z_i \mid w]\). The recentered instrument \(\tilde z_i = z_i - \mu_i\) is valid, because by iterated expectations and Equation 16.2,

\[ E[\tilde z_i \varepsilon_i] = E\big[\tilde z_i\, E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]\big] = E\big[E[\tilde z_i \mid w]\, E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]\big] = 0 . \]

Without recentering the same steps give \(E[z_i \varepsilon_i] = E[\mu_i E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]]\), which is not zero when the expected instrument is correlated with the predictable part of the error. Subtracting \(\mu_i\) and controlling for \(\mu_i\) are equivalent by Frisch–Waugh–Lovell. Computing \(\mu_i\) requires the design: how the shocks could have been drawn. We redraw them from that design, or permute them if they are exchangeable, and average the formula over the draws.

Any recentered formula is valid, so we can pick the most precise. Borusyak and Hull (2026, Theorem 1) show it starts from the best prediction of the treatment, recentered:

\[ \tilde z = E[x \mid g, w] - E[x \mid w] . \tag{16.3}\]

The full optimal instrument adds two refinements, partly controlling for the predictable part of the error and GLS weighting, which need a model of the error. The authors drop them. Their algorithm predicts the treatment from its formula, recenters the prediction by redrawing the shocks, and uses the result as the instrument. When \(x_i\) depends only on \((g, w)\), the prediction is \(x_i\) itself.

16.2 Shift-share as a formula instrument

The Bartik instrument \(B_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k} g_k\) of Equation 15.2 is the linear formula, shares times shocks. It differs from Equation 16.3 in two ways.

The first is recentering. Suppose the industry shocks are exchangeable within period \(t\), with mean \(\bar g_t\), and let \(S_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k}\) be region \(\ell\)’s sum of shares. Then

\[ \mu_\ell = S_\ell\, \bar g_t . \tag{16.4}\]

If the shares sum to one, \(\mu_\ell = \bar g_t\) is common to all regions and period effects absorb it, so Bartik is already recentered. If they are incomplete, as with manufacturing shares, heavily manufacturing regions get a large instrument in every draw, and Bartik is biased unless we control for \(S_\ell\) interacted with period (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 2022).

The second is the formula. Take two regions with half their employment in electronics, and an import shock to electronics. Region A’s plants compete with Chinese imports. Region B’s make military components and do not. Bartik gives both half the shock, but only A’s import competition rises, so the instrument varies where the treatment does not. The best predictor gives A half the shock and B nothing, then recenters each by its own expected value. Both instruments are valid, and the second has the stronger first stage. When the treatment really is shares times shocks, the best predictor is Bartik itself and the two differ only by recentering. The formula matters when the treatment has more structure than Bartik uses: eligibility rules, export exposure, transport networks, nonlinear indices.

The formula instrument needs more inputs: the treatment’s formula and an explicit shock design. With heterogeneous effects, different instruments weight units differently, so a gain in precision is a gain for a different weighted average (Borusyak and Hull 2026, Proposition D1).

16.2.1 Why recenter

Bartik needs one of two assumptions. If the shares are as good as random given the controls (Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift 2020), Bartik is valid as it stands and recentering is not needed. If only the shocks are random (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 2022), the shares may be correlated with the error. Writing each shock as the mean shock plus a deviation splits the instrument in two:

\[ B_\ell = S_\ell\, \bar g_t + \sum_k s_{\ell k}\,(g_k - \bar g_t) . \tag{16.5}\]

The first part is the expected instrument of Equation 16.4. It depends only on how much exposure the region has, so with incomplete shares a region with more exposure gets a larger value in every draw, and this part is correlated with the error whenever exposure is. The second part depends on whether the region’s industries received larger or smaller shocks than average. If the shocks are independent of the shares and the errors, the deviations average to zero whatever the shares are, so this part is uncorrelated with the error. Recentering keeps only the second part. When the shares sum to one, the first part is \(\bar g_t\) for every region and the period effects absorb it.

This gives the formula instrument four advantages. Its validity rests only on the shocks, such as a policy choice or a foreign supply shock, which are often easier to defend than the shares. The design has to be written down, so the assumption is explicit and can be varied, as the two ADH designs below show. The instrument can use exposure to gain power, because the best predictor tracks the treatment more closely than a shock-only index. And it is not tied to a linear formula or to independent units: it covers eligibility rules, networks and nonlinear indices, with shocks shared by many units.

16.3 Simulation

The aim is to compare a shock-only instrument with the recentered treatment when we know the true effect. There are 50 states and 10,000 people. Person \(i\) lives in state \(s(i)\) and has income \(\text{inc}_i\), in multiples of the poverty line, drawn from a log-normal distribution with median 1.4. The shock is the states’ policy: each state \(k\) draws an eligibility threshold \(T_k\) from \(\{0.5, 1, 1.38, 2\}\), each value with probability 1/4, independently across states. Eligibility and the outcome are

\[ x_i = 1[\text{inc}_i < T_{s(i)}], \qquad y_i = 0.3\, x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i = -0.5 \log \text{inc}_i + \alpha_{s(i)} + \nu_i , \tag{16.6}\]

where \(\alpha_k \sim N(0, 0.3^2)\) is a state effect and \(\nu_i \sim N(0, 1)\). The true effect is 0.3. In the notation of Equation 16.2, the shocks \(g\) are the 50 thresholds and the predetermined variables \(w\) are the incomes and states. Poorer people are more often eligible and have higher errors, so eligibility is correlated with the error through income and OLS is biased upward. The thresholds are drawn independently of incomes and errors, so Equation 16.2 holds.

Expected eligibility \(\mu_i\) is the probability that person \(i\) is eligible over the possible thresholds, holding income fixed. It is the share of the four thresholds above their income: 1 below 0.5, 0.75 between 0.5 and 1, 0.5 between 1 and 1.38, 0.25 between 1.38 and 2, and 0 above 2. It depends on income only, because every state draws from the same four values. Given income, the recentered treatment \(x_i - \mu_i\) moves only with the draw, which is why it is exogenous. A person with income 1.2 has \(\mu_i = 0.5\). The instrument is 0.5 if their state drew 1.38 or 2 and −0.5 if it drew 0.5 or 1, with equal chance whatever their error. People below 0.5 or above 2 always have zero and carry no identifying variation.

We compare four estimators, all with standard errors clustered by state.

  1. OLS of \(y_i\) on \(x_i\). This is the same as instrumenting \(x_i\) with itself, the best predictor without recentering. It is biased.
  2. The simulated instrument of Currie and Gruber (1996). They apply each state’s rules to one fixed national sample and use the share of that sample who would be eligible as the state’s instrument. The same population is run through every state’s rules, so the instrument measures the generosity of the rules and not the composition of the state’s residents. Here the national sample is all 10,000 people, and everyone in a state gets the same value. It is valid.
  3. The recentered treatment, instrumenting \(x_i\) with \(x_i - \mu_i\). It varies within states, among people whose income lies between the lowest and highest thresholds. It is valid.
  4. OLS of \(y_i\) on \(x_i\) and \(\mu_i\). Instrumenting \(x_i\) with \(x_i - \mu_i\) while controlling for \(\mu_i\) reduces to this regression (Borusyak and Hull 2026, footnote 8), so it is the controlled form of 3. It is valid.

The Monte Carlo is design-based. We generate the people once: their incomes, states and errors are the same in every replication. In each of 1,000 replications we draw new thresholds for the 50 states, recompute eligibility and outcomes, and fit the four estimators. The only randomness across replications is the policy draw, which is what the identifying assumption treats as random, so the spread of the estimates across replications measures each estimator’s precision under that design. The people and the 1,000 × 50 thresholds are stored as CSVs so the Julia companion computes the same numbers.

Code
people <- read_csv("data/recenter_people.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
draws  <- as.matrix(read_csv("data/recenter_policy_draws.csv",
                             show_col_types = FALSE)[, -1])
pool <- c(0.5, 1.0, 1.38, 2.0)
beta_true <- 0.3
mu    <- sapply(people$income, function(v) mean(v < pool))  # expected eligibility
gener <- sapply(pool, function(t) mean(people$income < t))  # state generosity

coef_x <- function(f) {
  nm <- intersect(c("fit_x", "x"), names(coef(f)))[1]
  c(est = unname(coef(f)[nm]), se = unname(se(f)[nm]))
}

one_draw <- function(r) {
  thr <- draws[r, people$state]
  d <- mutate(people, x = as.numeric(income < thr), y = beta_true * x + eps,
              z_sim = gener[match(thr, pool)], z_rc = x - mu, mu = mu)
  fits <- list(feols(y ~ x, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z_sim, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z_rc, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ x + mu, d, cluster = ~state))
  rbind(sapply(fits, coef_x),
        r2 = c(NA, cor(d$x, d$z_sim)^2, cor(d$x, d$z_rc)^2, NA))
}

       coverage = apply(abs(mc["est", , ] - beta_true) <= 1.96 * mc["se", , ], 1, mean),
       first_stage_R2 = apply(mc["r2", , ], 1, mean)) |>

The table gives each estimator’s mean and standard deviation over the 1,000 replications, the share of clustered 95% intervals that contain 0.3, and the average first-stage \(R^2\). OLS averages 0.637 against a true 0.3, and its intervals never contain 0.3. The three other estimators are centred on 0.3, with coverage of 92.7% to 93.7%, slightly below 95% with 50 clusters. They differ in precision. The simulated instrument has a standard deviation of 0.167 and a first-stage \(R^2\) of 0.26. It gives everyone in a state the same value, so it cannot separate people whose eligibility depends on the threshold from people who are eligible, or ineligible, under every threshold. The recentered treatment has a standard deviation of 0.086, half as large, and an \(R^2\) of 0.59, because it uses that variation within states. Its controlled form has 0.080.

In real data, Borusyak and Hull (2026) instrument Medicaid eligibility in the 2014 ACA expansion with actual minus expected eligibility, treating expansion as random within the governor’s party. The first-stage partial \(R^2\) rises from 0.01 to 0.80 over the simulated instrument and the standard error on crowd-out falls by 44%. Most of the gain comes from the 18% of the sample whose eligibility the expansion could change; outside it, the recentered instrument is zero, and in the full sample without an exposure control it is less precise than the simulated one (Borusyak and Hull 2026, Table A4). Their 2012–2013 placebo on private insurance is −0.011, half the main estimate of −0.023 (Table A2).

16.4 The China shock

In ADH the shocks are at the industry-period level: the growth of Chinese imports into eight other high-income countries, per US worker, in 397 manufacturing industries over 1990–2000 and 2000–2007. ADH argue they are exogenous because the growth came from China’s side, through reform, rising productivity and WTO accession, and because imports into other countries do not respond to US demand. The argument is weaker for demand or technology shocks shared by all rich countries, and the identifying variation is concentrated in a few industries such as computers, toys and audio equipment (Goldsmith-Pinkham, Sorkin, and Swift 2020).

All four regressions below use the same instrument and differ only in what they control for. The treatment, US import exposure per worker, is shares times US import growth, and US imports also reflect US demand. Replacing them with the other-country shocks gives the prediction, which is the Bartik instrument itself, so the formula-instrument approach does not change the instrument. It changes the control. The shares, lagged employment shares in the 722 commuting zones, are incomplete, so the expected instrument \(\mu_\ell\) varies across zones, and controlling for it is equivalent to subtracting it. Its form depends on the shock design.

  • Design 1: the 397 shocks are exchangeable within each period. Every industry then has the same expected shock, the period mean \(\bar g_t\), which is 4.8 in the first period and 17.3 in the second, and Equation 16.4 gives \(\mu_\ell = S_\ell \bar g_t\). Only a zone’s manufacturing intensity is treated as predictable.
  • Design 2: the shocks are exchangeable only within each of the 20 two-digit SIC sectors in each period. Each industry’s expected shock is its sector’s mean \(\bar g_{mt}\), and \(\mu_\ell = \sum_m S_{\ell m} \bar g_{mt}\), where \(S_{\ell m}\) is the zone’s share in sector \(m\). A zone’s sector mix is also treated as predictable: a zone heavy in electronics is expected to receive a large instrument.

ADH control for the start-of-period manufacturing share with one coefficient for both periods, which approximates design 1’s \(\mu_\ell\). The second row drops that control, so nothing recenters the instrument, and it is valid only if the shares are exogenous. All fits use ADH’s other controls, census-division effects, population weights and standard errors clustered by state, with the GPSS replication files.

The first row reproduces ADH’s −0.596: an increase in import exposure of $1,000 per worker over a decade lowers the manufacturing employment share by about 0.6 percentage points. Without the manufacturing-share control the estimate is −0.712.

Each of the last two rows removes one source of variation. ADH’s estimate partly compares zones with more and less manufacturing across the two periods: because the mean shock more than triples, a manufacturing-heavy zone gets a much larger instrument in the second period whichever industries China hit. Controlling for design 1’s \(\mu_\ell\) removes that comparison, and the estimate falls to −0.267 (0.087). ADH’s control is close to \(S_\ell\), with a correlation of 0.92, but one coefficient cannot absorb a term whose slope more than triples between periods. Design 2 also removes the comparison across sectors, such as electronics and toys against food and paper, and the estimate falls to −0.144 (0.117).

Whether the removed comparisons are confounded is the substantive question. If manufacturing-heavy zones declined faster in the 2000s for other reasons, such as automation, the design 1 control is needed and ADH overstate the effect. But the average shock is itself part of the China shock, since Chinese imports rose across nearly all of manufacturing. Designs 1 and 2 treat that average as predictable and use only the variation in which industries China hit harder than average, so they cannot measure an effect that works through manufacturing as a whole. ADH’s −0.596 does use it, and it is valid if the shares are exogenous given the controls. The data cannot tell us which assumption holds. Every estimate is negative, and recentering shows how much of the size depends on the assumption.

16.4.1 A two-zone example

To see what the manufacturing-share control does, take two zones and three manufacturing industries in one period. We observe one set of shocks. The design describes how they could have turned out: suppose each industry could have received a China shock of 30, in thousands of dollars of imports per worker, with probability 1/3, independently, and 0 otherwise, so the expected shock is \(\bar g = 10\). This is a simpler version of design 1, which reshuffles the observed shocks across industries. Zone A has a share of 0.1 in each of the three industries, so \(S_A = 0.3\). Zone B has a share of 0.1 in one of them, so \(S_B = 0.1\). To keep the example short, suppose US import exposure equals the Bartik measure \(B_\ell\), so the first stage is one and the instrument for exposure is \(B_\ell - \mu_\ell\). The exposures take these values:

Zone Shocked industries Probability \(B_\ell\) \(B_\ell - \mu_\ell\)
A 0 8/27 0 −3
A 1 12/27 3 0
A 2 6/27 6 3
A 3 1/27 9 6
B 0 2/3 0 −1
B 1 1/3 3 2

By Equation 16.4, \(\mu_A = 0.3 \times 10 = 3\) and \(\mu_B = 0.1 \times 10 = 1\), which are the probability-weighted averages of each zone’s exposures. Let the change in the manufacturing employment share, in percentage points, be

\[ y_\ell = -0.6 B_\ell - 10 S_\ell . \]

Each $1,000 of exposure per worker lowers the share by 0.6 points, and manufacturing-heavy zones decline by a further 10 points per unit of manufacturing share for other reasons, such as automation. In the most likely draw A has one shocked industry and B has none:

\[ y_A - y_B = -0.6 (3 - 0) - 10 (0.3 - 0.1) = -1.8 - 2 = -3.8 . \]

The raw slope is

\[ \frac{-3.8}{3} = -1.27 = -0.6 - \frac{2}{3} . \]

A declines 2 points more than B in every draw because it has more manufacturing. A also has more exposure in most draws, so the raw comparison counts this gap as an effect of imports, and the effect looks larger than it is. Pooling all draws of the two zones with equal weight, the raw slope is −0.8. Each zone’s \(B_\ell - \mu_\ell\) averages zero over the draws, so its covariance with \(S_\ell\) is zero, and using it as the instrument gives exactly −0.6. Since \(\mu_\ell = 10 S_\ell\), controlling for \(S_\ell\) does the same.

With two periods the mean shock changes, and \(\mu_\ell = S_\ell \bar g_t\) has a different slope on \(S_\ell\) in each period. With \(\bar g_t\) equal to 4.8 and 17.3, a single coefficient on \(S_\ell\) cannot match both, and part of the decline gap stays in the estimate. This is the difference between ADH’s control and design 1.

16.5 Spillovers: deworming

Miguel and Kremer (2004) study a deworming programme in 75 primary schools in Busia, Kenya. The schools were listed alphabetically within strata and assigned in turn to three groups of 25, which started treatment in different years. Worms spread between children, so treating one school can lower infection at schools nearby. To measure this, they count the pupils at treated schools within 3 km of each school, and they control for the total number of pupils within 3 km.

Let \(D_j\) be 1 if school \(j\) is treated, \(n_j\) its number of pupils, \(p_j\) the probability that it is treated, and \(j \sim i\) mean that \(j\) is another school within 3 km of school \(i\). The spillover exposure of school \(i\) and its expected value are

\[ x_i = \sum_{j \sim i} n_j D_j, \qquad \mu_i = \sum_{j \sim i} n_j p_j . \tag{16.7}\]

The shocks \(g\) are the assignments, and the predetermined variables \(w\) are the locations and pupil counts. Assignment is random, but a school in a dense area has more treated neighbours in every draw. If density also affects the outcome, through crowding or sanitation, the exposure is correlated with the error. In the first year one third of the schools were treated, so \(p_j = 1/3\) for every school and \(\mu_i\) is one third of the total pupils within 3 km. Miguel and Kremer’s total-pupils control is therefore the recentering. The exposure is the treatment itself, as eligibility was in the simulation, so the estimator is OLS controlling for \(\mu_i\), estimator 4 above. If the treatment probability differed across schools, for example by stratum, the control would have to be \(\mu_i\) and not the total pupils.

A second simulation checks this in a district like Busia. We place 75 schools in a 30 km by 30 km district, half around a town and half spread over the rest, and give each about 400 pupils. In each draw exactly 25 schools are treated, so \(p_j = 1/3\). Let \(N_i\) be the total pupils at other schools within 3 km and \(\eta_i \sim N(0, 0.05^2)\) a school error. The outcome, school participation, is

\[ y_i = 0.05 D_i + 0.03 x_i - 0.02 N_i + \eta_i , \tag{16.8}\]

with \(x_i\) and \(N_i\) in thousands of pupils. Treatment raises participation by 5 points at the school and by 3 points per thousand treated pupils nearby. Schools in dense areas have lower participation for other reasons. As before, the schools, their pupils and errors are fixed, and the 1,000 replications redraw which 25 schools are treated. We regress \(y_i\) on \(D_i\) and on (a) \(x_i\), (b) \(x_i - \mu_i\), or (c) \(x_i\) and \(N_i\), with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

Code
schools <- read_csv("data/deworm_schools.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
treat   <- as.matrix(read_csv("data/deworm_draws.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)[, -1])
near <- 1 * (as.matrix(dist(schools[, c("east", "north")])) < 3)  # within 3 km
diag(near) <- 0
N  <- drop(near %*% schools$pupils) / 1000   # total pupils nearby, thousands
mu <- N / 3                                  # expected treated pupils nearby

one_assign <- function(r) {
  D <- treat[r, ]
  d <- tibble(D = D, x = drop(near %*% (schools$pupils * D)) / 1000, N = N,
              x_rc = x - mu, y = 0.05 * D + 0.03 * x - 0.02 * N + schools$eta)
  fits <- list(feols(y ~ D + x, d, vcov = "hetero"),
               feols(y ~ D + x_rc, d, vcov = "hetero"),
               feols(y ~ D + x + N, d, vcov = "hetero"))
  sapply(fits, function(f) c(est = unname(coef(f)[3]), se = unname(se(f)[3])))
}
dw <- simplify2array(mclapply(seq_len(nrow(treat)), one_assign, mc.cores = 6))

Without the control, the spillover estimate averages −0.024 against a true 0.03, and its intervals never contain 0.03. Dense schools have more treated neighbours and lower participation, and the regression assigns that gap to the spillover. Both recentered versions are centred on 0.03. Regressing on \(x_i - \mu_i\) alone leaves \(-0.02 N_i\) in the error, so its standard deviation is 0.038, about twice the 0.020 of (c). Neighbouring schools share that error and the same treated schools, so the robust intervals of (b) contain 0.03 only 43% of the time. With the control for nearby pupils the remaining error is independent across schools, and coverage is 92%.

16.5.1 A two-school example

Take two untreated schools whose neighbours each have 400 pupils, and treat each neighbour independently with probability 1/3. School A is in town and has three neighbours within 3 km, so \(N_A = 1.2\) thousand. School B is rural and has one neighbour, so \(N_B = 0.4\). The number of A’s neighbours treated is binomial, so the exposures take these values:

School Treated neighbours Probability \(x\) \(x - \mu\)
A 0 8/27 0 −0.4
A 1 12/27 0.4 0
A 2 6/27 0.8 0.4
A 3 1/27 1.2 0.8
B 0 2/3 0 −0.133
B 1 1/3 0.4 0.267

Each school’s expected exposure is its exposure averaged over the draws, weighted by their probabilities. For A,

\[ \mu_A = \frac{0 \times 8 + 0.4 \times 12 + 0.8 \times 6 + 1.2 \times 1}{27} = \frac{10.8}{27} = 0.4 , \]

which is the 1.2 thousand pupils nearby times the treatment probability 1/3. For B, \(\mu_B = 0 \times 2/3 + 0.4 \times 1/3 = 0.133\). The town school expects more treated neighbours because it has more neighbours.

In the most likely draw A has one treated neighbour and B has none. With \(\eta_i\) set to zero, Equation 16.8 gives

\[ y_A - y_B = 0.03 (0.4 - 0) - 0.02 (1.2 - 0.4) = 0.012 - 0.016 = -0.004 . \]

The spillover raises A by 0.012 relative to B, and density lowers it by 0.016. A’s participation is 1.6 points lower than B’s in every draw because it has more pupils nearby. A also has more treated neighbours in most draws, so the raw comparison counts this gap as an effect of exposure. Its slope is

\[ \frac{y_A - y_B}{x_A - x_B} = \frac{-0.004}{0.4} = -0.01 = \frac{0.012}{0.4} - \frac{0.016}{0.4} = 0.03 - 0.04 . \]

The first term is the true spillover. The second is the bias, the density gap divided by the exposure gap, and in this draw it is large enough to flip the sign.

This is omitted-variable bias. Density affects participation, and density is correlated with exposure, because exposure is built from nearby pupils. If either link were absent, there would be no bias.

The formula instrument uses \(x - \mu\), how lucky each school was relative to its own expectation, as the instrument for \(x\):

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\text{cov}(y, x - \mu)}{\text{cov}(x, x - \mu)}, \qquad \text{cov}(y, x - \mu) = 0.03 \, \text{cov}(x, x - \mu) - 0.02 \, \text{cov}(N, x - \mu) . \]

The second term is zero. \(N\) is fixed for each school, and each school’s \(x - \mu\) averages zero over its draws; for A, \((-0.4 \times 8 + 0 \times 12 + 0.4 \times 6 + 0.8 \times 1)/27 = 0\). The ratio is then exactly 0.03. Raw \(x\) does not have this property, because A’s exposure is high in most draws and A is the dense school. Pooling all draws of the two schools with equal weight, the covariance of \(x\) with \(N\) is 0.053 and the variance of \(x\) is 0.089, so the slope of \(y\) on \(x\) is \((0.03 \times 0.089 - 0.02 \times 0.053)/0.089 = 0.018\). The covariance of \(x - \mu\) with \(N\) is 0, and the formula instrument gives 0.03.

The argument uses nothing specific to density. Any fixed characteristic of a school, such as local wealth or sanitation, has zero covariance with \(x - \mu\), because \(x - \mu\) comes only from the draw. We do not need to know which fixed variables affect the outcome, or to measure them. We need only \(\mu\), which comes from the design.

With 75 schools and one draw, the covariance of \(N\) with \(x - \mu\) is close to zero but not exactly zero, which is the extra noise of estimator (b). Controlling for \(N_i\), which here is the same as controlling for \(\mu_i\), removes the density term exactly. The comparison is then between schools with the same number of pupils nearby, which is why (c) is tighter.

16.6 Summary

Shift-share is one formula instrument, with the formula and the design fixed by convention. Any formula of shocks and exposure is valid once its expected value over the shock design is subtracted, and the most efficient formula is the recentered best prediction of the treatment. Applied to Bartik, this has two consequences. When the shares do not sum to one, the regression must control for the sum of shares times the period’s mean shock. When the treatment depends on the shocks through more than shares times shocks, a prediction built from the treatment’s own formula is a stronger instrument than Bartik. In the ADH data the first consequence alone moves the estimate from −0.60 to −0.27. The same logic covers spillovers. A school’s expected exposure to treated neighbours is the treatment probability times its nearby pupils, which is the control Miguel and Kremer used.

Many treatments are computed from a formula that combines a random shock with
non-random exposure. Medicaid eligibility is a state's rules applied to a
person's income. Import exposure is national industry shocks applied to a
region's industry mix. The Bartik instrument of the previous chapter is one
such formula. This chapter covers the general version of Borusyak and Hull
[@borusyak-hull-2023], their result on which formula is best
[@borusyak-hull-2026], and what both mean for shift-share.

## Formula instruments

Unit $i$ has outcome $y_i$ and treatment $x_i$, with a constant effect $\beta$:

$$
y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i .
$$ {#eq-ri-model}

The treatment is $x_i = h_i(g, w, u)$, where $h_i$ is a known function, $g$
collects the random shocks, $w$ collects predetermined variables such as
income or industry shares, and $u$ collects anything else. The identifying
assumption puts all the randomness on the shocks [@borusyak-hull-2026,
Assumption 1]:

$$
E[\varepsilon_i \mid g, w] = E[\varepsilon_i \mid w] .
$$ {#eq-ri-exog}

The error may depend on $w$ in any way. Poorer people may have worse
outcomes, and regions with more manufacturing may have been declining anyway.

A formula instrument is $z_i = f_i(g, w)$ for a fixed function $f_i$. Its
expected value over the shock draws, holding $w$ fixed, is the expected
instrument $\mu_i = E[z_i \mid w]$. The recentered instrument
$\tilde z_i = z_i - \mu_i$ is valid, because by iterated expectations and
@eq-ri-exog,

$$
E[\tilde z_i \varepsilon_i]
= E\big[\tilde z_i\, E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]\big]
= E\big[E[\tilde z_i \mid w]\, E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]\big] = 0 .
$$

Without recentering the same steps give $E[z_i \varepsilon_i] = E[\mu_i
E[\varepsilon_i \mid w]]$, which is not zero when the expected instrument is
correlated with the predictable part of the error. Subtracting $\mu_i$ and
controlling for $\mu_i$ are equivalent by Frisch–Waugh–Lovell. Computing
$\mu_i$ requires the design: how the shocks could have been drawn. We redraw
them from that design, or permute them if they are exchangeable, and average
the formula over the draws.

Any recentered formula is valid, so we can pick the most precise.
@borusyak-hull-2026 [Theorem 1] show it starts from the best prediction of the
treatment, recentered:

$$
\tilde z = E[x \mid g, w] - E[x \mid w] .
$$ {#eq-ri-optimal}

The full optimal instrument adds two refinements, partly controlling for the
predictable part of the error and GLS weighting, which need a model of the
error. The authors drop them. Their algorithm predicts the treatment from its
formula, recenters the prediction by redrawing the shocks, and uses the result
as the instrument. When $x_i$ depends only on $(g, w)$, the prediction is
$x_i$ itself.

## Shift-share as a formula instrument

The Bartik instrument $B_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k} g_k$ of @eq-shift-share-iv-1
is the linear formula, shares times shocks. It differs from
@eq-ri-optimal in two ways.

The first is recentering. Suppose the industry shocks are exchangeable within
period $t$, with mean $\bar g_t$, and let $S_\ell = \sum_k s_{\ell k}$ be
region $\ell$'s sum of shares. Then

$$
\mu_\ell = S_\ell\, \bar g_t .
$$ {#eq-ri-ss-mu}

If the shares sum to one, $\mu_\ell = \bar g_t$ is common to all regions and
period effects absorb it, so Bartik is already recentered. If they are
incomplete, as with manufacturing shares, heavily manufacturing regions get a
large instrument in every draw, and Bartik is biased unless we control for
$S_\ell$ interacted with period [@borusyak-hull-jaravel-2022].

The second is the formula. Take two regions with half their employment in
electronics, and an import shock to electronics. Region A's plants compete
with Chinese imports. Region B's make military components and do not. Bartik
gives both half the shock, but only A's import competition rises, so the
instrument varies where the treatment does not. The best predictor gives A
half the shock and B nothing, then recenters each by its own expected value.
Both instruments are valid, and the second has the stronger first stage. When
the treatment really is shares times shocks, the best predictor is Bartik
itself and the two differ only by recentering. The formula matters when the
treatment has more structure than Bartik uses: eligibility rules, export
exposure, transport networks, nonlinear indices.

|                     | Shift-share (Bartik)      | Recentered formula instrument          |
|---------------------|---------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Formula             | Shares times shocks       | Best prediction of the treatment       |
| Expected instrument | Usually not subtracted    | Subtracted, or controlled for          |
| Shock design        | Implicit                  | Written down to compute $\mu_\ell$     |
| Inputs              | Shares and shocks         | Treatment formula and shock design     |

The formula instrument needs more inputs: the treatment's formula and an explicit
shock design. With heterogeneous effects, different instruments weight units
differently, so a gain in precision is a gain for a different weighted
average [@borusyak-hull-2026, Proposition D1].

### Why recenter

Bartik needs one of two assumptions. If the shares are as good as random given
the controls [@goldsmith-pinkham-sorkin-swift-2020], Bartik is valid as it
stands and recentering is not needed. If only the shocks are random [@borusyak-hull-jaravel-2022], the shares may be
correlated with the error. Writing each shock as the mean shock plus a
deviation splits the instrument in two:

$$
B_\ell = S_\ell\, \bar g_t + \sum_k s_{\ell k}\,(g_k - \bar g_t) .
$$ {#eq-ri-bartik-split}

The first part is the expected instrument of @eq-ri-ss-mu. It depends only on
how much exposure the region has, so with incomplete shares a region with more
exposure gets a larger value in every draw, and this part is correlated with
the error whenever exposure is. The second part depends on whether the
region's industries received larger or smaller shocks than average. If the
shocks are independent of the shares and the errors, the deviations average to
zero whatever the shares are, so this part is uncorrelated with the error.
Recentering keeps only the second part. When the shares sum to one, the first
part is $\bar g_t$ for every region and the period effects absorb it.

This gives the formula instrument four advantages. Its validity rests only on the shocks, such as a policy choice or a foreign supply shock, which are often easier to defend than the shares. The
design has to be written down, so the assumption is explicit and can be
varied, as the two ADH designs below show. The instrument can use exposure to
gain power, because the best predictor tracks the treatment more closely than
a shock-only index. And it is not tied to a linear formula or to independent
units: it covers eligibility rules, networks and nonlinear indices, with
shocks shared by many units.

## Simulation

The aim is to compare a shock-only instrument with the recentered treatment
when we know the true effect. There are 50 states and 10,000 people. Person
$i$ lives in state $s(i)$ and has income $\text{inc}_i$, in multiples of the
poverty line, drawn from a log-normal distribution with median 1.4. The shock
is the states' policy: each state $k$ draws an eligibility threshold $T_k$
from $\{0.5, 1, 1.38, 2\}$, each value with probability 1/4, independently
across states. Eligibility and the outcome are

$$
x_i = 1[\text{inc}_i < T_{s(i)}], \qquad
y_i = 0.3\, x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad
\varepsilon_i = -0.5 \log \text{inc}_i + \alpha_{s(i)} + \nu_i ,
$$ {#eq-ri-dgp}

where $\alpha_k \sim N(0, 0.3^2)$ is a state effect and $\nu_i \sim N(0, 1)$.
The true effect is 0.3. In the notation of @eq-ri-exog, the shocks $g$ are the
50 thresholds and the predetermined variables $w$ are the incomes and states.
Poorer people are more often eligible and have higher errors, so eligibility
is correlated with the error through income and OLS is biased upward. The
thresholds are drawn independently of incomes and errors, so @eq-ri-exog
holds.

Expected eligibility $\mu_i$ is the probability that person $i$ is eligible
over the possible thresholds, holding income fixed. It is the share of the
four thresholds above their income: 1 below 0.5, 0.75 between 0.5 and 1, 0.5
between 1 and 1.38, 0.25 between 1.38 and 2, and 0 above 2. It depends on
income only, because every state draws from the same four values. Given
income, the recentered treatment $x_i - \mu_i$ moves only with the draw, which
is why it is exogenous. A person with income 1.2 has $\mu_i = 0.5$. The
instrument is 0.5 if their state drew 1.38 or 2 and −0.5 if it drew 0.5 or 1,
with equal chance whatever their error. People below 0.5 or above 2 always
have zero and carry no identifying variation.

We compare four estimators, all with standard errors clustered by state.

1. OLS of $y_i$ on $x_i$. This is the same as instrumenting $x_i$ with
   itself, the best predictor without recentering. It is biased.
2. The simulated instrument of @currie-gruber-1996. They apply each state's
   rules to one fixed national sample and use the share of that sample who
   would be eligible as the state's instrument. The same population is run
   through every state's rules, so the instrument measures the generosity of
   the rules and not the composition of the state's residents. Here the
   national sample is all 10,000 people, and everyone in a state gets the
   same value. It is valid.
3. The recentered treatment, instrumenting $x_i$ with $x_i - \mu_i$. It
   varies within states, among people whose income lies between the lowest
   and highest thresholds. It is valid.
4. OLS of $y_i$ on $x_i$ and $\mu_i$. Instrumenting $x_i$ with $x_i - \mu_i$
   while controlling for $\mu_i$ reduces to this regression
   [@borusyak-hull-2026, footnote 8], so it is the controlled form of 3. It
   is valid.

The Monte Carlo is design-based. We generate the people once: their incomes,
states and errors are the same in every replication. In each of 1,000
replications we draw new thresholds for the 50 states, recompute eligibility
and outcomes, and fit the four estimators. The only randomness across
replications is the policy draw, which is what the identifying assumption
treats as random, so the spread of the estimates across replications measures
each estimator's precision under that design. The people and the 1,000 × 50
thresholds are stored as CSVs so the Julia companion computes the same
numbers.

```{r}
#| label: ri-mc
#| cache: true

people <- read_csv("data/recenter_people.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
draws  <- as.matrix(read_csv("data/recenter_policy_draws.csv",
                             show_col_types = FALSE)[, -1])
pool <- c(0.5, 1.0, 1.38, 2.0)
beta_true <- 0.3
mu    <- sapply(people$income, function(v) mean(v < pool))  # expected eligibility
gener <- sapply(pool, function(t) mean(people$income < t))  # state generosity

coef_x <- function(f) {
  nm <- intersect(c("fit_x", "x"), names(coef(f)))[1]
  c(est = unname(coef(f)[nm]), se = unname(se(f)[nm]))
}

one_draw <- function(r) {
  thr <- draws[r, people$state]
  d <- mutate(people, x = as.numeric(income < thr), y = beta_true * x + eps,
              z_sim = gener[match(thr, pool)], z_rc = x - mu, mu = mu)
  fits <- list(feols(y ~ x, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z_sim, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z_rc, d, cluster = ~state),
               feols(y ~ x + mu, d, cluster = ~state))
  rbind(sapply(fits, coef_x),
        r2 = c(NA, cor(d$x, d$z_sim)^2, cor(d$x, d$z_rc)^2, NA))
}

mc <- simplify2array(mclapply(seq_len(nrow(draws)), one_draw, mc.cores = 6))

tibble(estimator = c("OLS", "Simulated instrument", "Recentered treatment",
                     "OLS controlling for mu"),
       mean     = apply(mc["est", , ], 1, mean),
       sd       = apply(mc["est", , ], 1, sd),
       coverage = apply(abs(mc["est", , ] - beta_true) <= 1.96 * mc["se", , ], 1, mean),
       first_stage_R2 = apply(mc["r2", , ], 1, mean)) |>
  kable(digits = 3)
```

The table gives each estimator's mean and standard deviation over the 1,000
replications, the share of clustered 95% intervals that contain 0.3, and the
average first-stage $R^2$. OLS averages 0.637 against a true 0.3, and its
intervals never contain 0.3. The three other estimators are centred on 0.3,
with coverage of 92.7% to 93.7%, slightly below 95% with 50 clusters. They
differ in precision. The simulated instrument has a standard deviation of
0.167 and a first-stage $R^2$ of 0.26. It gives everyone in a state the same
value, so it cannot separate people whose eligibility depends on the
threshold from people who are eligible, or ineligible, under every threshold.
The recentered treatment has a standard deviation of 0.086, half as large, and
an $R^2$ of 0.59, because it uses that variation within states. Its controlled
form has 0.080.

In real data, @borusyak-hull-2026 instrument Medicaid eligibility in the 2014 ACA expansion
with actual minus expected eligibility, treating expansion as random within
the governor's party. The first-stage partial $R^2$ rises from 0.01 to 0.80
over the simulated instrument and the standard error on crowd-out falls by
44%. Most of the gain comes from the 18% of the sample whose eligibility the
expansion could change; outside it, the recentered instrument is zero, and in
the full sample without an exposure control it is less precise than the
simulated one [@borusyak-hull-2026, Table A4]. Their 2012–2013 placebo on
private insurance is −0.011, half the main estimate of −0.023 (Table A2).

## The China shock

In ADH the shocks are at the industry-period level: the growth of Chinese
imports into eight other high-income countries, per US worker, in 397
manufacturing industries over 1990–2000 and 2000–2007. ADH argue they are
exogenous because the growth came from China's side, through reform, rising
productivity and WTO accession, and because imports into other countries do
not respond to US demand. The argument is weaker for demand or technology
shocks shared by all rich countries, and the identifying variation is
concentrated in a few industries such as computers, toys and audio equipment
[@goldsmith-pinkham-sorkin-swift-2020].

All four regressions below use the same instrument and differ only in what
they control for. The treatment, US import exposure per worker, is shares
times US import growth, and US imports also reflect US demand. Replacing them
with the other-country shocks gives the prediction, which is the Bartik
instrument itself, so the formula-instrument approach does not change the
instrument. It changes the control. The shares, lagged employment shares in
the 722 commuting zones, are incomplete, so the expected instrument $\mu_\ell$
varies across zones, and controlling for it is equivalent to subtracting it.
Its form depends on the shock design.

- Design 1: the 397 shocks are exchangeable within each period. Every
  industry then has the same expected shock, the period mean $\bar g_t$, which
  is 4.8 in the first period and 17.3 in the second, and @eq-ri-ss-mu gives
  $\mu_\ell = S_\ell \bar g_t$. Only a zone's manufacturing intensity is
  treated as predictable.
- Design 2: the shocks are exchangeable only within each of the 20 two-digit
  SIC sectors in each period. Each industry's expected shock is its sector's
  mean $\bar g_{mt}$, and $\mu_\ell = \sum_m S_{\ell m} \bar g_{mt}$, where
  $S_{\ell m}$ is the zone's share in sector $m$. A zone's sector mix is also
  treated as predictable: a zone heavy in electronics is expected to receive a
  large instrument.

ADH control for the start-of-period manufacturing share with one coefficient
for both periods, which approximates design 1's $\mu_\ell$. The second row
drops that control, so nothing recenters the instrument, and it is valid only
if the shares are exogenous. All fits use ADH's other controls,
census-division effects, population weights and standard errors clustered by
state, with the GPSS replication files.


The first row reproduces ADH's −0.596: an increase in import exposure of
$1,000 per worker over a decade lowers the manufacturing employment share by
about 0.6 percentage points. Without the manufacturing-share control the
estimate is −0.712.

Each of the last two rows removes one source of variation. ADH's estimate
partly compares zones with more and less manufacturing across the two periods:
because the mean shock more than triples, a manufacturing-heavy zone gets a
much larger instrument in the second period whichever industries China hit.
Controlling for design 1's $\mu_\ell$ removes that comparison, and the estimate
falls to −0.267 (0.087). ADH's control is close to $S_\ell$, with a
correlation of 0.92, but one coefficient cannot absorb a term whose slope more
than triples between periods. Design 2 also removes the comparison across
sectors, such as electronics and toys against food and paper, and the estimate
falls to −0.144 (0.117).

Whether the removed comparisons are confounded is the substantive question.
If manufacturing-heavy zones declined faster in the 2000s for other reasons,
such as automation, the design 1 control is needed and ADH overstate the
effect. But the average shock is itself part of the China shock, since
Chinese imports rose across nearly all of manufacturing. Designs 1 and 2 treat
that average as predictable and use only the variation in which industries
China hit harder than average, so they cannot measure an effect that works
through manufacturing as a whole. ADH's −0.596 does use it, and it is valid if
the shares are exogenous given the controls. The data cannot tell us which
assumption holds. Every estimate is negative, and recentering shows how much
of the size depends on the assumption.

### A two-zone example

To see what the manufacturing-share control does, take two zones and three
manufacturing industries in one period. We observe one set of shocks. The
design describes how they could have turned out: suppose each industry could
have received a China shock of 30, in thousands of dollars of imports per
worker, with probability 1/3, independently, and 0 otherwise, so the expected
shock is $\bar g = 10$. This is a simpler version of design 1, which
reshuffles the observed shocks across industries. Zone A has a share of 0.1 in
each of the three industries, so $S_A = 0.3$. Zone B has a share of 0.1 in one
of them, so $S_B = 0.1$. To keep the example short, suppose US import exposure
equals the Bartik measure $B_\ell$, so the first stage is one and the
instrument for exposure is $B_\ell - \mu_\ell$. The exposures take these
values:

| Zone | Shocked industries | Probability | $B_\ell$ | $B_\ell - \mu_\ell$ |
|:----:|:------------------:|:-----------:|:--------:|:-------------------:|
| A | 0 | 8/27  | 0 | −3 |
| A | 1 | 12/27 | 3 | 0  |
| A | 2 | 6/27  | 6 | 3  |
| A | 3 | 1/27  | 9 | 6  |
| B | 0 | 2/3   | 0 | −1 |
| B | 1 | 1/3   | 3 | 2  |

By @eq-ri-ss-mu, $\mu_A = 0.3 \times 10 = 3$ and $\mu_B = 0.1 \times 10 = 1$,
which are the probability-weighted averages of each zone's exposures. Let the
change in the manufacturing employment share, in percentage points, be

$$
y_\ell = -0.6 B_\ell - 10 S_\ell .
$$

Each \$1,000 of exposure per worker lowers the share by 0.6 points, and
manufacturing-heavy zones decline by a further 10 points per unit of
manufacturing share for other reasons, such as automation. In the most likely
draw A has one shocked industry and B has none:

$$
y_A - y_B = -0.6 (3 - 0) - 10 (0.3 - 0.1) = -1.8 - 2 = -3.8 .
$$

The raw slope is

$$
\frac{-3.8}{3} = -1.27 = -0.6 - \frac{2}{3} .
$$

A declines 2 points more than B in every draw because it has more
manufacturing. A also has more exposure in most draws, so the raw comparison
counts this gap as an effect of imports, and the effect looks larger than it
is. Pooling all draws of the two zones with equal weight, the raw slope is
−0.8. Each zone's $B_\ell - \mu_\ell$ averages zero over the draws, so its
covariance with $S_\ell$ is zero, and using it as the instrument gives exactly
−0.6. Since $\mu_\ell = 10 S_\ell$, controlling for $S_\ell$ does the same.

With two periods the mean shock changes, and $\mu_\ell = S_\ell \bar g_t$ has a
different slope on $S_\ell$ in each period. With $\bar g_t$ equal to 4.8 and
17.3, a single coefficient on $S_\ell$ cannot match both, and part of the
decline gap stays in the estimate. This is the difference between ADH's
control and design 1.

## Spillovers: deworming

@miguel-kremer-2004 study a deworming programme in 75 primary schools in
Busia, Kenya. The schools were listed alphabetically within strata and
assigned in turn to three groups of 25, which started treatment in different
years. Worms spread between children, so treating one school can lower
infection at schools nearby. To measure this, they count the pupils at
treated schools within 3 km of each school, and they control for the total
number of pupils within 3 km.

Let $D_j$ be 1 if school $j$ is treated, $n_j$ its number of pupils, $p_j$
the probability that it is treated, and $j \sim i$ mean that $j$ is another
school within 3 km of school $i$. The spillover exposure of school $i$ and its
expected value are

$$
x_i = \sum_{j \sim i} n_j D_j, \qquad \mu_i = \sum_{j \sim i} n_j p_j .
$$ {#eq-ri-spill}

The shocks $g$ are the assignments, and the predetermined variables $w$ are the
locations and pupil counts. Assignment is random, but a school in a dense area
has more treated neighbours in every draw. If density also affects the
outcome, through crowding or sanitation, the exposure is correlated with the
error. In the first year one third of the schools were treated, so $p_j = 1/3$
for every school and $\mu_i$ is one third of the total pupils within 3 km.
Miguel and Kremer's total-pupils control is therefore the recentering. The
exposure is the treatment itself, as eligibility was in the simulation, so
the estimator is OLS controlling for $\mu_i$, estimator 4 above. If the
treatment probability differed across schools, for example by stratum, the
control would have to be $\mu_i$ and not the total pupils.

A second simulation checks this in a district like Busia. We place 75 schools
in a 30 km by 30 km district, half around a town and half spread over the
rest, and give each about 400 pupils. In each draw exactly 25 schools are
treated, so $p_j = 1/3$. Let $N_i$ be the total pupils at other schools within
3 km and $\eta_i \sim N(0, 0.05^2)$ a school error. The outcome, school
participation, is

$$
y_i = 0.05 D_i + 0.03 x_i - 0.02 N_i + \eta_i ,
$$ {#eq-ri-spill-dgp}

with $x_i$ and $N_i$ in thousands of pupils. Treatment raises participation by
5 points at the school and by 3 points per thousand treated pupils nearby.
Schools in dense areas have lower participation for other reasons. As before,
the schools, their pupils and errors are fixed, and the 1,000 replications
redraw which 25 schools are treated. We regress $y_i$ on $D_i$ and on (a)
$x_i$, (b) $x_i - \mu_i$, or (c) $x_i$ and $N_i$, with
heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

```{r}
#| label: ri-deworm
#| cache: true

schools <- read_csv("data/deworm_schools.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)
treat   <- as.matrix(read_csv("data/deworm_draws.csv", show_col_types = FALSE)[, -1])
near <- 1 * (as.matrix(dist(schools[, c("east", "north")])) < 3)  # within 3 km
diag(near) <- 0
N  <- drop(near %*% schools$pupils) / 1000   # total pupils nearby, thousands
mu <- N / 3                                  # expected treated pupils nearby

one_assign <- function(r) {
  D <- treat[r, ]
  d <- tibble(D = D, x = drop(near %*% (schools$pupils * D)) / 1000, N = N,
              x_rc = x - mu, y = 0.05 * D + 0.03 * x - 0.02 * N + schools$eta)
  fits <- list(feols(y ~ D + x, d, vcov = "hetero"),
               feols(y ~ D + x_rc, d, vcov = "hetero"),
               feols(y ~ D + x + N, d, vcov = "hetero"))
  sapply(fits, function(f) c(est = unname(coef(f)[3]), se = unname(se(f)[3])))
}
dw <- simplify2array(mclapply(seq_len(nrow(treat)), one_assign, mc.cores = 6))

tibble(estimator = c("(a) Exposure, no control", "(b) Recentered exposure",
                     "(c) Exposure, control for nearby pupils"),
       mean     = apply(dw["est", , ], 1, mean),
       sd       = apply(dw["est", , ], 1, sd),
       coverage = apply(abs(dw["est", , ] - 0.03) <= 1.96 * dw["se", , ], 1, mean)) |>
  kable(digits = 3)
```

Without the control, the spillover estimate averages −0.024 against a true
0.03, and its intervals never contain 0.03. Dense schools have more treated
neighbours and lower participation, and the regression assigns that gap to the
spillover. Both recentered versions are centred on 0.03. Regressing on
$x_i - \mu_i$ alone leaves $-0.02 N_i$ in the error, so its standard deviation
is 0.038, about twice the 0.020 of (c). Neighbouring schools share that error
and the same treated schools, so the robust intervals of (b) contain 0.03 only
43% of the time. With the control for nearby pupils the remaining error is
independent across schools, and coverage is 92%.

### A two-school example

Take two untreated schools whose neighbours each have 400 pupils, and treat
each neighbour independently with probability 1/3. School A is in town and has
three neighbours within 3 km, so $N_A = 1.2$ thousand. School B is rural and
has one neighbour, so $N_B = 0.4$. The number of A's neighbours treated is
binomial, so the exposures take these values:

| School | Treated neighbours | Probability | $x$ | $x - \mu$ |
|:------:|:------------------:|:-----------:|:---:|:---------:|
| A | 0 | 8/27  | 0   | −0.4   |
| A | 1 | 12/27 | 0.4 | 0      |
| A | 2 | 6/27  | 0.8 | 0.4    |
| A | 3 | 1/27  | 1.2 | 0.8    |
| B | 0 | 2/3   | 0   | −0.133 |
| B | 1 | 1/3   | 0.4 | 0.267  |

Each school's expected exposure is its exposure averaged over the draws,
weighted by their probabilities. For A,

$$
\mu_A = \frac{0 \times 8 + 0.4 \times 12 + 0.8 \times 6 + 1.2 \times 1}{27}
= \frac{10.8}{27} = 0.4 ,
$$

which is the 1.2 thousand pupils nearby times the treatment probability 1/3.
For B, $\mu_B = 0 \times 2/3 + 0.4 \times 1/3 = 0.133$. The town school
expects more treated neighbours because it has more neighbours.

In the most likely draw A has one treated neighbour and B has none. With
$\eta_i$ set to zero, @eq-ri-spill-dgp gives

$$
y_A - y_B = 0.03 (0.4 - 0) - 0.02 (1.2 - 0.4) = 0.012 - 0.016 = -0.004 .
$$

The spillover raises A by 0.012 relative to B, and density lowers it by
0.016. A's participation is 1.6 points lower than B's in every draw because it
has more pupils nearby. A also has more treated neighbours in most draws, so
the raw comparison counts this gap as an effect of exposure. Its slope is

$$
\frac{y_A - y_B}{x_A - x_B} = \frac{-0.004}{0.4} = -0.01
= \frac{0.012}{0.4} - \frac{0.016}{0.4} = 0.03 - 0.04 .
$$

The first term is the true spillover. The second is the bias, the density
gap divided by the exposure gap, and in this draw it is large enough to flip
the sign.

This is omitted-variable bias. Density affects participation, and density is
correlated with exposure, because exposure is built from nearby pupils. If
either link were absent, there would be no bias.

The formula instrument uses $x - \mu$, how lucky each school was relative to
its own expectation, as the instrument for $x$:

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\text{cov}(y, x - \mu)}{\text{cov}(x, x - \mu)}, \qquad
\text{cov}(y, x - \mu) = 0.03 \, \text{cov}(x, x - \mu) - 0.02 \, \text{cov}(N, x - \mu) .
$$

The second term is zero. $N$ is fixed for each school, and each school's
$x - \mu$ averages zero over its draws; for A,
$(-0.4 \times 8 + 0 \times 12 + 0.4 \times 6 + 0.8 \times 1)/27 = 0$. The
ratio is then exactly 0.03. Raw $x$ does not have this property, because A's
exposure is high in most draws and A is the dense school. Pooling all draws of
the two schools with equal weight, the covariance of $x$ with $N$ is 0.053 and
the variance of $x$ is 0.089, so the slope of $y$ on $x$ is
$(0.03 \times 0.089 - 0.02 \times 0.053)/0.089 = 0.018$. The covariance of
$x - \mu$ with $N$ is 0, and the formula instrument gives 0.03.

The argument uses nothing specific to density. Any fixed characteristic of a
school, such as local wealth or sanitation, has zero covariance with
$x - \mu$, because $x - \mu$ comes only from the draw. We do not need to know
which fixed variables affect the outcome, or to measure them. We need only
$\mu$, which comes from the design.

With 75 schools and one draw, the covariance of $N$ with $x - \mu$ is close to
zero but not exactly zero, which is the extra noise of estimator (b).
Controlling for $N_i$, which here is the same as controlling for $\mu_i$,
removes the density term exactly. The comparison is then between schools with
the same number of pupils nearby, which is why (c) is tighter.

## Summary

Shift-share is one formula instrument, with the formula and the design fixed
by convention. Any formula of shocks and exposure is valid once its expected
value over the shock design is subtracted, and the most efficient formula is
the recentered best prediction of the treatment. Applied to Bartik, this has two consequences. When the shares do not sum to one, the regression must control for the sum of shares times the period's mean shock. When the treatment depends on the shocks through more than shares times shocks, a prediction built from the treatment's own formula is a stronger instrument than Bartik. In the ADH data the first consequence alone moves the estimate from −0.60 to −0.27. The same logic covers spillovers. A school's expected exposure to treated neighbours is the treatment probability times its nearby pupils, which is the control Miguel and Kremer used.

see the previous chapter, [Shift-Share Instrumental Variables](shift-share-iv.qmd).
