The previous chapters often treat identification as yes or no. Applied work is usually less clean. We may believe the adjustment set is good, but still worry about some remaining confounding. Sensitivity analysis asks a practical question: how strong would unmeasured confounding have to be to change the conclusion?

Related reading: An extended treatment of the Cinelli-Hazlett robustness value and benchmark interpretations appears in the companion blog chapter on sensitivity analysis of Topics on Econometrics and Causal Inference.

Here I use four methods:

  1. Cinelli-Hazlett (2020) omitted-variable bias bounds — for OLS regression estimates, quantifies how strong an omitted variable would need to be (in terms of partial \(R^2\) with treatment and outcome) to bring the estimate to zero.
  2. E-values (VanderWeele-Ding 2017) — for risk ratios, the minimum strength of association (on the risk-ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to fully explain the observed association.
  3. Rosenbaum bounds — for matched estimators, the magnitude of non-random treatment assignment within matched pairs that would render the test result insignificant.
  4. The marginal sensitivity model (Tan 2006; Zhao, Small and Bhattacharya 2019; Dorn and Guo 2023) — for weighting estimators, the factor by which an unmeasured confounder may move each unit’s odds of treatment before the confidence interval for the ATE includes zero.

5.2 Cinelli-Hazlett OVB bounds

The framework is Cinelli and Hazlett (2020).

sensemakr implements the Cinelli-Hazlett (2020) approach. The key inputs are:

  • a fitted regression
  • the treatment variable name
  • one or more benchmark covariates whose strength serves as a reference for how strong a hypothetical unobserved confounder might be
Code
sens <- sensemakr(
  model = ols,
  treatment = "qsmk",
  benchmark_covariates = c("smokeintensity", "smokeyrs"),
  kd = 1:3,                 # benchmark: 1×, 2×, 3× as strong as the benchmark
  ky = 1:3,
  q  = 1,                   # the "bias proportion" at which to flag a problem
  alpha = 0.05,
  reduce = TRUE
)

The summary tells us:

  • Robustness value (RV): the minimum strength of association (in terms of partial \(R^2\)) that an unmeasured confounder would need with both the treatment and the outcome to reduce the estimate to zero. Higher = more robust.
  • RV_q: the strength needed to “explain away” a fraction \(q\) of the estimate (here \(q = 1\) means complete explanation).
  • Benchmark comparisons: how the RV compares to multiples of the observed covariates.

For this regression the partial \(R^2\) of quitting with weight change is 3.65%. The robustness value at \(q=1\) is 17.67%. A confounder that explains 17.67% of the residual variance of both quitting and weight change, after the measured covariates, brings the estimate to zero; one that explains less than that of both cannot. It is an equal-strength threshold. A confounder can still explain the estimate away with one partial \(R^2\) below 17.67% if the other is large enough, because the bias depends on their product. To leave it merely statistically indistinguishable from zero at the 5% level, 13.47% would be enough.

The benchmark rows put those numbers on a scale. Smoking intensity explains 1.43% of the residual variance of quitting and 0.10% of the residual variance of weight change. A confounder three times as strong on both margins would move the estimate from 3.381 to 3.182, with a 95% interval of \([2.30, 4.07]\). None of the measured covariates comes near 17.67% on either margin, and the bounds built from them leave the estimate well above zero, so the estimate is robust to a confounder resembling the ones we already control for.

The horizontal axis is the confounder’s partial \(R^2\) with the treatment, the vertical axis its partial \(R^2\) with the outcome, and the contours give the adjusted estimate at each combination. The benchmark diamonds mark 1x, 2x and 3x smoking intensity and years smoked. 5.3 E-values for risk ratios

The E-value is VanderWeele and Ding (2017).

For binary outcomes and risk-ratio-type estimands, VanderWeele and Ding (2017) propose the E-value: the minimum strength of association on the risk-ratio scale that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the treatment and the outcome to fully explain the observed risk ratio.

To illustrate, we dichotomize weight change into any gain versus none, fit the risk ratio by Poisson regression with a log link (Zou 2004) on the same covariates, and take the confidence interval from robust standard errors:

Code
nhefs$gain <- as.numeric(nhefs$wt82_71 > 0)

rr_fit <- glm(gain ~ qsmk + sex + age + race + education + smokeintensity +
                     smokeyrs + exercise + active + wt71,
              data = nhefs, family = poisson(link = "log"))
rr_summary <- tidy(rr_fit, exp = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

rr_est <- exp(coef(rr_fit)["qsmk"])
# The Poisson likelihood is misspecified for a binary outcome (the Zou 2004
# log-binomial-via-Poisson trick), so the model-based variance is wrong.
# Use robust (sandwich/HC) standard errors for the RR confidence interval.
rr_ci  <- lmtest::coefci(rr_fit, parm = "qsmk",
                         vcov. = sandwich::vcovHC(rr_fit, type = "HC0"))
rr_lo  <- exp(rr_ci[1])
rr_hi  <- exp(rr_ci[2])

cat(sprintf("Adjusted RR (quit vs not): %.3f  [%.3f, %.3f]\n",
            rr_est, rr_lo, rr_hi))
Adjusted RR (quit vs not): 1.218  [1.135, 1.307]
Code
ev <- evalues.RR(est = rr_est, lo = rr_lo, hi = rr_hi)
print(ev)
            point    lower    upper

The E-value reports two numbers:

  • E-value (point estimate): an unmeasured confounder associated with both treatment and outcome by risk ratios of this value, above and beyond the measured confounders, could fully explain away the observed effect; one weaker than this on both could not. Like the RV, it is an equal-strength threshold, and a confounder stronger on one association can be weaker on the other.
  • E-value (confidence interval): the minimum strength needed to shift the lower confidence bound to 1.

The adjusted risk ratio is 1.218, with a 95% interval of \([1.135, 1.307]\). Quitters are about 22% more likely to gain any weight. The E-value for the point estimate is 1.73: an unmeasured confounder with risk-ratio associations of 1.73 with both quitting and weight gain, beyond the nine measured covariates, could account for the whole association, and one below 1.73 on both could not. The E-value for the confidence limit is 1.53, the strength that would push the lower bound to 1.

An E-value much greater than 1 suggests the result is hard to explain by unmeasured confounding. An E-value close to 1 means a modest unmeasured confounder could overturn the conclusion. At 1.73 this result sits in between: a confounder as strong as that is not implausible, but it would have to be unrelated to everything already in the model.

The useful part of the E-value is that it is on the risk-ratio scale. It can be explained without specifying a model for the unobserved confounder.

5.4 Rosenbaum bounds (matched estimators)

The sensitivity model and the bound computed below are Rosenbaum’s (2002).

For matching estimators, Rosenbaum bounds (Rosenbaum 2002) provide a sensitivity analysis based on the maximum deviation from random assignment within matched pairs.

Suppose units in a matched pair are observationally identical, but one might still be more likely than the other to receive treatment due to unobserved factors. Let \(\Gamma\) bound the ratio of treatment odds between the two units of a pair: \(\Gamma = 1\) means matched pairs are perfectly randomised (both units equally likely to be the treated one); \(\Gamma = 2\) means hidden bias could make one unit’s odds of treatment up to twice the other’s. Formally, let units \(i\) and \(j\) share the same observed covariates and let \(\pi_i\) and \(\pi_j\) be their probabilities of treatment given everything that affects treatment, observed or not. The model assumes

\[ \frac{1}{\Gamma} \le \frac{\pi_i (1-\pi_j)}{\pi_j (1-\pi_i)} \le \Gamma \tag{5.1}\]

(Rosenbaum 1987; Rosenbaum 2002, ch. 4).

Rosenbaum bounds compute the range of \(p\)-values for the matched test under each value of \(\Gamma\). The critical \(\Gamma\) is the smallest value at which the test becomes insignificant.

Here is a worked example on the same NHEFS data. We fit the propensity score by logistic regression on the nine baseline covariates, match each of the 403 quitters to its nearest unmatched control on that score, take the paired differences in weight change, and compute the upper-bound Wilcoxon signed-rank \(p\)-value on a grid \(\Gamma \in [1, 4]\) in steps of 0.25:

Code
# Estimate propensity score
ps_fit <- glm(qsmk ~ sex + age + race + education + smokeintensity +
                     smokeyrs + exercise + active + wt71,
              data = nhefs, family = binomial)
nhefs$ps <- predict(ps_fit, type = "response")

# Simple greedy 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching on the propensity score, without
# replacement. Nothing here is random, but the result depends on row order:
# treated units are matched in data order, and each takes its control out of the
# pool, so later units get worse matches. MatchIt's greedy nearest-neighbour
# matching has the same dependence; it only makes the order an explicit choice
# (m.order) along with tie handling. Check balance and match distances either way.
treated_idx <- which(nhefs$qsmk == 1)
control_idx <- which(nhefs$qsmk == 0)

matched_pairs <- data.frame(t_idx = integer(), c_idx = integer())
available_controls <- control_idx
for (ti in treated_idx) {
  if (length(available_controls) == 0) break
  dists <- abs(nhefs$ps[ti] - nhefs$ps[available_controls])
  best  <- which.min(dists)
  matched_pairs <- rbind(matched_pairs,
                         data.frame(t_idx = ti,
                                    c_idx = available_controls[best]))
  available_controls <- available_controls[-best]
}

# Compute paired differences in outcome

               nhefs$wt82_71[matched_pairs$c_idx]

Code
# Rosenbaum bound calculation: range of p-values for Wilcoxon signed-rank
# under each Gamma. We compute a large-sample NORMAL APPROXIMATION to the
# weighted Bernoulli rank sum directly rather than using a package, to keep the
# dependency footprint small. With 403 pairs it tracks the canonical bound
# closely, but it is an approximation: there is no exact Poisson-binomial tail
# and no continuity correction here. It assumes a positive effect and is an
# upper bound only where the observed rank sum is above its mean under Gamma.
rosenbaum_pvalue <- function(diffs, gamma) {
  # The Wilcoxon signed-rank protocol discards exact-zero paired differences
  # before ranking (a zero carries no information about the sign) and
  # reduces the effective sample size accordingly; including them would
  # skew both the rank sum and its null variance.
  diffs <- diffs[diffs != 0]
  abs_d <- abs(diffs)
  signs <- sign(diffs)
  ranks <- rank(abs_d)
  # Under Gamma, P(positive sign) is bounded between 1/(1+Γ) and Γ/(1+Γ).
  # Upper-bound p-value: assume P(positive) = Γ/(1+Γ) (against null of zero)
  p_pos_upper <- gamma / (1 + gamma)
  # Test statistic: sum of positive ranks
  T_obs <- sum(ranks[signs > 0])
  n <- length(diffs)
  mu_upper  <- p_pos_upper * sum(ranks)
  var_upper <- p_pos_upper * (1 - p_pos_upper) * sum(ranks^2)
  z <- (T_obs - mu_upper) / sqrt(var_upper)
  1 - pnorm(z)
}

gammas <- seq(1, 4, by = 0.25)
pvals  <- sapply(gammas, function(g) rosenbaum_pvalue(paired_diff, g))

5.5 The marginal sensitivity model (weighting estimators)

The model below is Tan’s (2006), in the form used by Zhao, Small, and Bhattacharya (2019). The sharp bounds are those of Dorn and Guo (2023).

Rosenbaum’s model compares the two units of a matched pair. Weighting estimators have no pairs. They weight each unit by the inverse of its estimated propensity score, so the natural bound compares each unit’s true propensity with that score.

Let \(Z\) be the treatment indicator, \(X\) the observed covariates and \(U\) an unobserved confounder. The nominal propensity score \(e(x) = P(Z = 1 \mid X = x)\) is the one we estimate. The true propensity score \(e_0(x, u) = P(Z = 1 \mid X = x, U = u)\) also conditions on \(U\). Let \(\Lambda \ge 1\) be the sensitivity parameter. The marginal sensitivity model assumes, for all \(x\) and \(u\),

\[ \frac{1}{\Lambda} \le \frac{e_0(x,u) / \{1 - e_0(x,u)\}}{e(x) / \{1 - e(x)\}} \le \Lambda \tag{5.2}\]

(Zhao, Small, and Bhattacharya 2019, Definition 3.1). The confounder may move a unit’s odds of treatment at most a factor \(\Lambda\) away from what the covariates predict. At \(\Lambda = 1\) there is no hidden bias.

The bound on the odds becomes a bound on each weight. Write the odds of \(e_0\) as the odds of \(e\) times a factor between \(1/\Lambda\) and \(\Lambda\), and solve for \(1/e_0\). A treated unit’s weight must lie in

\[ 1 + \frac{1/e(x) - 1}{\Lambda} \le \frac{1}{e_0(x,u)} \le 1 + \Lambda \{1/e(x) - 1\}. \tag{5.3}\]

A control’s weight \(1/\{1 - e_0(x,u)\}\) obeys the same interval with \(e\) replaced by \(1 - e\). At \(\Lambda = 1\) both intervals shrink to the usual weights.

We estimate the mean outcome in each arm by the stabilized (Hajek) IPW estimator, the weighted mean of \(Y\) in that arm, and the ATE by the difference of the two means. Under the model we no longer know the weights, only their intervals. Zhao, Small, and Bhattacharya (2019, eq. 4.12) take the smallest and largest weighted means over all weights in the intervals. The largest mean puts the top weight on units with the largest outcomes, so it is found by sorting \(Y\) and scanning one cutoff.

These bounds are valid but not sharp. Some weights in the intervals cannot be the true ones, because the true inverse propensities must average to the nominal ones at each value of \(X\). Dorn and Guo (2023, Theorem 1, Proposition 2) impose this. In the sharp upper bound for an arm, the weight jumps from the low to the high end of Equation 5.3 at the conditional \(\tau\)-quantile of \(Y\) given \(X\), with \(\tau = \Lambda / (1 + \Lambda)\), not at one cutoff for the whole sample. Their Lemma 1 computes it with one weighted linear quantile regression of \(Y\) on \(X\) in the arm. The lower bound applies the same step to \(-Y\). Subtracting the arm bounds gives bounds on the ATE, and these are sharp (Dorn and Guo 2023, Theorem 2). For a confidence interval we follow Zhao, Small, and Bhattacharya (2019, eq. 4.11). In each bootstrap sample we refit the propensity score and recompute the bounds. The interval runs from the 2.5th percentile of the lower bounds to the 97.5th percentile of the upper bounds.

The functions below implement both bounds. We checked them outside the book. At \(\Lambda = 1\) both return the Hajek estimate. The sorting scan matches a search over every vertex of the weight box. Lemma 1 matches a direct linear programming solution of Dorn and Guo’s problem. In a simulation where the model holds with \(\Lambda = 1.5\), the interval at \(\Lambda = 1.5\) covered the true ATE in 299 of 300 replications.

Code
# Interval for a unit's inverse true propensity under the marginal sensitivity
# model. `e` is the nominal probability of the unit's own arm.
msm_weight_box <- function(e, Lambda) {
  r <- 1 / e - 1
  list(lo = 1 + r / Lambda, hi = 1 + Lambda * r)
}

# Zhao, Small and Bhattacharya (2019, eq. 4.12): smallest and largest weighted
# mean of y over weights in [lo, hi]. The maximum gives weight hi to the k
# largest outcomes and lo to the rest, for some k; we scan every k.
zsb_bounds <- function(y, lo, hi) {
  ext <- function(y) {
    o <- order(y, decreasing = TRUE)
    y <- y[o]; lo <- lo[o]; hi <- hi[o]
    num <- sum(lo * y) + c(0, cumsum((hi - lo) * y))
    den <- sum(lo) + c(0, cumsum(hi - lo))
    max(num / den)
  }
  c(lower = -ext(-y), upper = ext(y))
}

# Dorn and Guo (2023, Lemma 1): sharp upper bound from one weighted linear
# quantile regression of y on (1, X) at tau = Lambda / (1 + Lambda), with
# weights (1 - e) / e. The weight is 1 + Lambda (1 - e) / e above the fitted
# quantile and 1 + (1 - e) / (e Lambda) below it.
qb_upper <- function(y, X, e, Lambda) {
  tau <- Lambda / (1 + Lambda)
  G   <- cbind(1, X)
  fit <- suppressWarnings(          # "solution may be nonunique" is harmless here
    quantreg::rq.wfit(G, y, tau = tau, weights = (1 - e) / e, method = "br"))
  q <- drop(G %*% fit$coefficients)
  r <- y - q
  (sum(r * (1 + Lambda^sign(r) * (1 - e) / e)) + sum(q / e)) / sum(1 / e)
}

qb_bounds <- function(y, X, e, Lambda) {
  c(lower = -qb_upper(-y, X, e, Lambda), upper = qb_upper(y, X, e, Lambda))
}

# ATE bounds: treated arm with e, control arm with 1 - e, then subtract.
msm_ate_bounds <- function(y, z, X, e, Lambda, method = c("qb", "zsb")) {
  method <- match.arg(method)
  arm <- function(idx, p) {
    if (method == "qb") return(qb_bounds(y[idx], X[idx, , drop = FALSE], p, Lambda))
    b <- msm_weight_box(p, Lambda)
    zsb_bounds(y[idx], b$lo, b$hi)
  }
  t1 <- arm(z == 1, e[z == 1])
  t0 <- arm(z == 0, 1 - e[z == 0])
  c(lower = unname(t1["lower"] - t0["upper"]),
    upper = unname(t1["upper"] - t0["lower"]))
}

How does \(\Lambda\) relate to Rosenbaum’s \(\Gamma\)? Each model contains the other at a different value of its parameter (Zhao, Small, and Bhattacharya 2019, Proposition 7.1). Suppose Equation 5.1 holds with \(\Gamma\). The nominal \(e(x)\) is the average of \(e_0(x, U)\) over \(U\), so its odds lie between the smallest and largest true odds at \(x\). Every true odds is then within a factor \(\Gamma\) of the odds of \(e(x)\), and Equation 5.2 holds with \(\Lambda = \Gamma\). In the other direction, two units may sit a factor \(\Lambda\) above and below the odds of \(e(x)\), so Equation 5.2 with \(\Lambda\) gives Equation 5.1 with \(\Gamma = \Lambda^2\). A conclusion that survives \(\Lambda = 1.5\) therefore survives every confounder Rosenbaum’s model allows at \(\Gamma = 1.5\). The two analyses above are still not directly comparable. The matched analysis tests a sharp null for the ATT with randomization inference; the weighting analysis bounds the ATE and uses the bootstrap.

The four analyses do not share a scale. \(\Gamma\) and \(\Lambda\) bound odds ratios of treatment, the E-value bounds a risk ratio, and the robustness value is a partial \(R^2\). They also ask different questions: \(\Gamma\), \(\Lambda\) and the E-value ask when the result stops being significant, the robustness value asks when the point estimate reaches zero. Reporting one of them is normal practice; the numbers should not be averaged or compared directly.

5.6 What about partial identification?

The bounding tradition here starts with Manski (1990); Lee (2009) gives the treatment-selection bounds used in applied work.

When sensitivity analysis shows the point estimate is fragile, partial identification is often the next step. Instead of one estimate, report bounds under weaker assumptions.

The Manski (1990) “no-assumption” bounds say:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ATE} &\ge \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}1]P(D{=}1) - \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}0]P(D{=}0) - K\,P(D{=}1), \\ \text{ATE} &\le \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}1]P(D{=}1) - \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}0]P(D{=}0) + K\,P(D{=}0). \end{aligned} \tag{5.4}\]

Each potential-outcome mean is split by the law of total expectation, filling in the unobserved arm with its worst/best case. With outcomes bounded in \(Y \in [0, K]\), this is a single interval of width exactly \(K\) — bounded, but often too wide to be useful. Tighter bounds come from adding structure to a specific problem: Lee (2009) bounds address sample selection/attrition (the outcome is observed only for selected units) by combining random assignment with monotone selection and trimming the over-selected arm. For IV settings, Mourifié and Wan (2017) provide a test of the LATE identifying assumptions (instrument validity plus monotonicity).

These bounds usually complement a point estimate. They are useful when the point estimate depends too much on an untestable assumption. The marginal sensitivity model sits between the two extremes: at \(\Lambda = 1\) it gives the point estimate, and as \(\Lambda\) grows its sharp bounds widen toward the no-assumption interval.

5.7 Summary

  • Sensitivity analysis asks how strong hidden confounding must be to change the result.
  • Cinelli-Hazlett bounds are useful for regression estimates.
  • E-values are useful for risk ratios.
  • Rosenbaum bounds are useful for matched pairs.
  • The marginal sensitivity model is useful for weighting estimators; Dorn and Guo’s quantile-balancing bounds are sharp and need one quantile regression per arm.
  • Observational causal estimates should usually include at least one sensitivity analysis.

The previous chapters often treat identification as yes or no. Applied work
is usually less clean. We may believe the adjustment set is good, but still
worry about some remaining confounding. Sensitivity analysis asks a practical
question: how strong would unmeasured confounding have to be to change the
conclusion?

> **Related reading**: An extended treatment of the Cinelli-Hazlett
> robustness value and benchmark interpretations appears in the
> [companion blog chapter on sensitivity analysis](https://xiangao.github.io/blog_book/sensitivity-analysis.html)
> of *Topics on Econometrics and Causal Inference*.

Here I use four methods:

1. **Cinelli-Hazlett (2020) omitted-variable bias bounds** — for OLS
   regression estimates, quantifies how strong an omitted variable
   would need to be (in terms of partial $R^2$ with treatment and outcome)
   to bring the estimate to zero.
2. **E-values (VanderWeele-Ding 2017)** — for risk ratios, the minimum
   strength of association (on the risk-ratio scale) that an unmeasured
   confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to fully explain
   the observed association.
3. **Rosenbaum bounds** — for matched estimators, the magnitude of
   non-random treatment assignment within matched pairs that would render
   the test result insignificant.
4. **The marginal sensitivity model (Tan 2006; Zhao, Small and Bhattacharya
   2019; Dorn and Guo 2023)** — for weighting estimators, the factor by which
   an unmeasured confounder may move each unit's odds of treatment before the
   confidence interval for the ATE includes zero.

## Cinelli-Hazlett OVB bounds

The framework is Cinelli and Hazlett [-@cinelli-hazlett-2020].

`sensemakr` implements the Cinelli-Hazlett (2020) approach. The key inputs
are:

- a fitted regression
- the treatment variable name
- one or more *benchmark covariates* whose strength serves as a reference
  for how strong a hypothetical unobserved confounder might be

```{r}
#| label: sensemakr
#| cache: true

sens <- sensemakr(
  model = ols,
  treatment = "qsmk",
  benchmark_covariates = c("smokeintensity", "smokeyrs"),
  kd = 1:3,                 # benchmark: 1×, 2×, 3× as strong as the benchmark
  ky = 1:3,
  q  = 1,                   # the "bias proportion" at which to flag a problem
  alpha = 0.05,
  reduce = TRUE
)

summary(sens)
```

The summary tells us:

- **Robustness value (RV)**: the minimum strength of association (in terms of
  partial $R^2$) that an unmeasured confounder would need with both the
  treatment and the outcome to reduce the estimate to zero. Higher = more
  robust.
- **RV_q**: the strength needed to "explain away" a fraction $q$ of the
  estimate (here $q = 1$ means complete explanation).
- **Benchmark comparisons**: how the RV compares to multiples of the
  observed covariates.

For this regression the partial $R^2$ of quitting with weight change is 3.65%.
The robustness value at $q=1$ is 17.67%. A confounder that explains 17.67% of the
residual variance of both quitting and weight change, after the measured
covariates, brings the estimate to zero; one that explains less than that of
both cannot. It is an equal-strength threshold. A confounder can still explain
the estimate away with one partial $R^2$ below 17.67% if the other is large
enough, because the bias depends on their product. To leave it
merely statistically indistinguishable from zero at the 5% level, 13.47% would
be enough.

The benchmark rows put those numbers on a scale. Smoking intensity explains
1.43% of the residual variance of quitting and 0.10% of the residual variance
of weight change. A confounder three times as strong on both margins would move
the estimate from 3.381 to 3.182, with a 95% interval of $[2.30, 4.07]$. None
of the measured covariates comes near 17.67% on either margin, and the bounds
built from them leave the estimate well above zero, so the estimate is robust to a
confounder resembling the ones we already control for.

The same information is easier to read as a contour plot. The horizontal axis
is the confounder's partial $R^2$ with the treatment, the vertical axis its
partial $R^2$ with the outcome, and the contours give the adjusted estimate at
each combination. The benchmark diamonds mark 1x, 2x and 3x smoking intensity
and years smoked. All six sit very close to the origin, so we cut the axes at
0.06 and 0.006 to keep their labels apart; the robustness value of 0.177 that
would drive the estimate to zero is far outside this window.

## E-values for risk ratios

The E-value is VanderWeele and Ding [-@vanderweele-ding-2017].

For binary outcomes and risk-ratio-type estimands, VanderWeele and Ding
(2017) propose the **E-value**: the minimum strength of association on the
risk-ratio scale that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both
the treatment and the outcome to fully explain the observed risk ratio.

To illustrate, we dichotomize weight change into any gain versus none, fit the
risk ratio by Poisson regression with a log link [@zou-2004] on the same covariates,
and take the confidence interval from robust standard errors:

```{r}
#| label: evalue
#| cache: true

nhefs$gain <- as.numeric(nhefs$wt82_71 > 0)

rr_fit <- glm(gain ~ qsmk + sex + age + race + education + smokeintensity +
                     smokeyrs + exercise + active + wt71,
              data = nhefs, family = poisson(link = "log"))
rr_summary <- tidy(rr_fit, exp = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE)

rr_est <- exp(coef(rr_fit)["qsmk"])
# The Poisson likelihood is misspecified for a binary outcome (the Zou 2004
# log-binomial-via-Poisson trick), so the model-based variance is wrong.
# Use robust (sandwich/HC) standard errors for the RR confidence interval.
rr_ci  <- lmtest::coefci(rr_fit, parm = "qsmk",
                         vcov. = sandwich::vcovHC(rr_fit, type = "HC0"))
rr_lo  <- exp(rr_ci[1])
rr_hi  <- exp(rr_ci[2])

cat(sprintf("Adjusted RR (quit vs not): %.3f  [%.3f, %.3f]\n",
            rr_est, rr_lo, rr_hi))

ev <- evalues.RR(est = rr_est, lo = rr_lo, hi = rr_hi)
print(ev)
```

The E-value reports two numbers:

- **E-value (point estimate)**: an unmeasured confounder associated with both
  treatment and outcome by risk ratios of this value, above and beyond the
  measured confounders, could fully explain away the observed effect; one
  weaker than this on both could not. Like the RV, it is an equal-strength
  threshold, and a confounder stronger on one association can be weaker on the
  other.
- **E-value (confidence interval)**: the minimum strength needed to shift
  the lower confidence bound to 1.

The adjusted risk ratio is 1.218, with a 95% interval of $[1.135, 1.307]$.
Quitters are about 22% more likely to gain any weight. The E-value for the
point estimate is 1.73: an unmeasured confounder with risk-ratio associations
of 1.73 with both quitting and weight gain, beyond the nine measured covariates,
could account for the whole association, and one below 1.73 on both could not. The E-value for
the confidence limit is 1.53, the strength that would push the lower bound to 1.

An E-value much greater than 1 suggests the result is hard to explain by
unmeasured confounding. An E-value close to 1 means a modest unmeasured
confounder could overturn the conclusion. At 1.73 this result sits in between:
a confounder as strong as that is not implausible, but it would have to be
unrelated to everything already in the model.

The useful part of the E-value is that it is on the risk-ratio scale. It can
be explained without specifying a model for the unobserved confounder.

## Rosenbaum bounds (matched estimators)

The sensitivity model and the bound computed below are Rosenbaum's [-@rosenbaum-2002].

For matching estimators, **Rosenbaum bounds** (Rosenbaum 2002) provide a
sensitivity analysis based on the maximum deviation from random assignment
within matched pairs.

Suppose units in a matched pair are observationally identical, but one
might still be more likely than the other to receive treatment due to
unobserved factors. Let $\Gamma$ bound the ratio of treatment odds between
the two units of a pair: $\Gamma = 1$ means matched pairs are perfectly
randomised (both units equally likely to be the treated one); $\Gamma = 2$
means hidden bias could make one unit's odds of treatment up to twice the
other's. Formally, let units $i$ and $j$ share the same observed covariates and
let $\pi_i$ and $\pi_j$ be their probabilities of treatment given everything
that affects treatment, observed or not. The model assumes

$$
\frac{1}{\Gamma} \le \frac{\pi_i (1-\pi_j)}{\pi_j (1-\pi_i)} \le \Gamma
$$ {#eq-sensitivity-rosenbaum}

[@rosenbaum-1987; @rosenbaum-2002, ch. 4].

Rosenbaum bounds compute the range of $p$-values for the matched test
under each value of $\Gamma$. The **critical $\Gamma$** is the smallest
value at which the test becomes insignificant.

Here is a worked example on the same NHEFS data. We fit the propensity score by
logistic regression on the nine baseline covariates, match each of the 403
quitters to its nearest unmatched control on that score, take the paired
differences in weight change, and compute the upper-bound Wilcoxon
signed-rank $p$-value on a grid $\Gamma \in [1, 4]$ in steps of 0.25:

```{r}
#| label: rosenbaum
#| cache: true

# Estimate propensity score
ps_fit <- glm(qsmk ~ sex + age + race + education + smokeintensity +
                     smokeyrs + exercise + active + wt71,
              data = nhefs, family = binomial)
nhefs$ps <- predict(ps_fit, type = "response")

# Simple greedy 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching on the propensity score, without
# replacement. Nothing here is random, but the result depends on row order:
# treated units are matched in data order, and each takes its control out of the
# pool, so later units get worse matches. MatchIt's greedy nearest-neighbour
# matching has the same dependence; it only makes the order an explicit choice
# (m.order) along with tie handling. Check balance and match distances either way.
treated_idx <- which(nhefs$qsmk == 1)
control_idx <- which(nhefs$qsmk == 0)

matched_pairs <- data.frame(t_idx = integer(), c_idx = integer())
available_controls <- control_idx
for (ti in treated_idx) {
  if (length(available_controls) == 0) break
  dists <- abs(nhefs$ps[ti] - nhefs$ps[available_controls])
  best  <- which.min(dists)
  matched_pairs <- rbind(matched_pairs,
                         data.frame(t_idx = ti,
                                    c_idx = available_controls[best]))
  available_controls <- available_controls[-best]
}

# Compute paired differences in outcome
paired_diff <- nhefs$wt82_71[matched_pairs$t_idx] -
               nhefs$wt82_71[matched_pairs$c_idx]
cat(sprintf("N matched pairs: %d\n", nrow(matched_pairs)))
cat(sprintf("Mean paired difference (matched ATT): %.3f kg\n",
            mean(paired_diff)))

# Rosenbaum bound calculation: range of p-values for Wilcoxon signed-rank
# under each Gamma. We compute a large-sample NORMAL APPROXIMATION to the
# weighted Bernoulli rank sum directly rather than using a package, to keep the
# dependency footprint small. With 403 pairs it tracks the canonical bound
# closely, but it is an approximation: there is no exact Poisson-binomial tail
# and no continuity correction here. It assumes a positive effect and is an
# upper bound only where the observed rank sum is above its mean under Gamma.
rosenbaum_pvalue <- function(diffs, gamma) {
  # The Wilcoxon signed-rank protocol discards exact-zero paired differences
  # before ranking (a zero carries no information about the sign) and
  # reduces the effective sample size accordingly; including them would
  # skew both the rank sum and its null variance.
  diffs <- diffs[diffs != 0]
  abs_d <- abs(diffs)
  signs <- sign(diffs)
  ranks <- rank(abs_d)
  # Under Gamma, P(positive sign) is bounded between 1/(1+Γ) and Γ/(1+Γ).
  # Upper-bound p-value: assume P(positive) = Γ/(1+Γ) (against null of zero)
  p_pos_upper <- gamma / (1 + gamma)
  # Test statistic: sum of positive ranks
  T_obs <- sum(ranks[signs > 0])
  n <- length(diffs)
  mu_upper  <- p_pos_upper * sum(ranks)
  var_upper <- p_pos_upper * (1 - p_pos_upper) * sum(ranks^2)
  z <- (T_obs - mu_upper) / sqrt(var_upper)
  1 - pnorm(z)
}

gammas <- seq(1, 4, by = 0.25)
pvals  <- sapply(gammas, function(g) rosenbaum_pvalue(paired_diff, g))

result <- tibble(Gamma = gammas, p_value_upper = pvals)
knitr::kable(result, digits = 4,
             caption = "Upper-bound p-values from Rosenbaum sensitivity analysis")
```

Matching gives 403 pairs, one per quitter, with a mean paired difference of
2.926 kg. The upper-bound $p$-value is 0.0334 at $\Gamma = 1.50$ and 0.2905 at
$\Gamma = 1.75$, so the crossing of 0.05 falls between those two grid points.

The **critical $\Gamma$** is the value at which the upper-bound $p$-value
crosses 0.05. If it is around 2 or higher, the result is robust to moderate
hidden bias. If it is close to 1, even small unmeasured confounding could
overturn the conclusion. We read it off the grid:

```{r}
#| label: critical-gamma
#| cache: true

sig_idx <- which(result$p_value_upper > 0.05)
critical_gamma <- if (length(sig_idx) > 0) result$Gamma[min(sig_idx)] else NA
if (is.na(critical_gamma)) {
  cat(sprintf("No Gamma up to %.2f pushed the p-value above 0.05 -- the result is robust across the whole grid tested; extend `gammas` to find the true crossing.\n", max(result$Gamma)))
} else {
  cat(sprintf("Critical Gamma (p > 0.05): %.2f\n", critical_gamma))
}
```

The critical $\Gamma$ is 1.75. This is the first value *on the 0.25 grid* at
which significance is lost; the true crossing lies between 1.50 and 1.75, and a
finer grid would locate it more precisely. The convention in applied work is
often to report the largest $\Gamma$ at which significance is retained, which
here is 1.50.

So hidden bias that made one member of a matched pair up to about 1.5 times
more likely to quit than the other would leave the result significant, and bias
beyond that would not.

## The marginal sensitivity model (weighting estimators)

The model below is Tan's [-@tan-2006-distributional], in the form used by
@zhao-small-bhattacharya-2019. The sharp bounds are those of @dorn-guo-2023.

Rosenbaum's model compares the two units of a matched pair. Weighting
estimators have no pairs. They weight each unit by the inverse of its
estimated propensity score, so the natural bound compares each unit's true
propensity with that score.

Let $Z$ be the treatment indicator, $X$ the observed covariates and $U$ an
unobserved confounder. The nominal propensity score $e(x) = P(Z = 1 \mid X = x)$
is the one we estimate. The true propensity score
$e_0(x, u) = P(Z = 1 \mid X = x, U = u)$ also conditions on $U$. Let
$\Lambda \ge 1$ be the sensitivity parameter. The marginal sensitivity model
assumes, for all $x$ and $u$,

$$
\frac{1}{\Lambda} \le \frac{e_0(x,u) / \{1 - e_0(x,u)\}}{e(x) / \{1 - e(x)\}} \le \Lambda
$$ {#eq-sensitivity-msm}

[@zhao-small-bhattacharya-2019, Definition 3.1]. The confounder may move a
unit's odds of treatment at most a factor $\Lambda$ away from what the
covariates predict. At $\Lambda = 1$ there is no hidden bias.

The bound on the odds becomes a bound on each weight. Write the odds of $e_0$
as the odds of $e$ times a factor between $1/\Lambda$ and $\Lambda$, and solve
for $1/e_0$. A treated unit's weight must lie in

$$
1 + \frac{1/e(x) - 1}{\Lambda} \le \frac{1}{e_0(x,u)} \le 1 + \Lambda \{1/e(x) - 1\}.
$$ {#eq-sensitivity-msm-weights}

A control's weight $1/\{1 - e_0(x,u)\}$ obeys the same interval with $e$
replaced by $1 - e$. At $\Lambda = 1$ both intervals shrink to the usual
weights.

We estimate the mean outcome in each arm by the stabilized (Hajek) IPW
estimator, the weighted mean of $Y$ in that arm, and the ATE by the
difference of the two means. Under the model we no longer know the weights,
only their intervals. @zhao-small-bhattacharya-2019 [eq. 4.12] take the
smallest and largest weighted means over all weights in the intervals. The
largest mean puts the top weight on units with the largest outcomes, so it is
found by sorting $Y$ and scanning one cutoff.

These bounds are valid but not sharp. Some weights in the intervals cannot be
the true ones, because the true inverse propensities must average to the
nominal ones at each value of $X$. @dorn-guo-2023 [Theorem 1, Proposition 2]
impose this. In the sharp upper bound for an arm, the weight jumps from the
low to the high end of @eq-sensitivity-msm-weights at the conditional
$\tau$-quantile of $Y$ given $X$, with $\tau = \Lambda / (1 + \Lambda)$, not at
one cutoff for the whole sample. Their Lemma 1 computes it with one weighted
linear quantile regression of $Y$ on $X$ in the arm. The lower bound applies
the same step to $-Y$. Subtracting the arm bounds gives bounds on the ATE, and
these are sharp [@dorn-guo-2023, Theorem 2]. For a confidence interval we
follow @zhao-small-bhattacharya-2019 [eq. 4.11]. In each bootstrap sample we
refit the propensity score and recompute the bounds. The interval runs from
the 2.5th percentile of the lower bounds to the 97.5th percentile of the upper
bounds.

The functions below implement both bounds. We checked them outside the book.
At $\Lambda = 1$ both return the Hajek estimate. The sorting scan matches a
search over every vertex of the weight box. Lemma 1 matches a direct linear
programming solution of Dorn and Guo's problem. In a simulation where the
model holds with $\Lambda = 1.5$, the interval at $\Lambda = 1.5$ covered the
true ATE in 299 of 300 replications.

```{r}
#| label: msm-functions
#| cache: true

# Interval for a unit's inverse true propensity under the marginal sensitivity
# model. `e` is the nominal probability of the unit's own arm.
msm_weight_box <- function(e, Lambda) {
  r <- 1 / e - 1
  list(lo = 1 + r / Lambda, hi = 1 + Lambda * r)
}

# Zhao, Small and Bhattacharya (2019, eq. 4.12): smallest and largest weighted
# mean of y over weights in [lo, hi]. The maximum gives weight hi to the k
# largest outcomes and lo to the rest, for some k; we scan every k.
zsb_bounds <- function(y, lo, hi) {
  ext <- function(y) {
    o <- order(y, decreasing = TRUE)
    y <- y[o]; lo <- lo[o]; hi <- hi[o]
    num <- sum(lo * y) + c(0, cumsum((hi - lo) * y))
    den <- sum(lo) + c(0, cumsum(hi - lo))
    max(num / den)
  }
  c(lower = -ext(-y), upper = ext(y))
}

# Dorn and Guo (2023, Lemma 1): sharp upper bound from one weighted linear
# quantile regression of y on (1, X) at tau = Lambda / (1 + Lambda), with
# weights (1 - e) / e. The weight is 1 + Lambda (1 - e) / e above the fitted
# quantile and 1 + (1 - e) / (e Lambda) below it.
qb_upper <- function(y, X, e, Lambda) {
  tau <- Lambda / (1 + Lambda)
  G   <- cbind(1, X)
  fit <- suppressWarnings(          # "solution may be nonunique" is harmless here
    quantreg::rq.wfit(G, y, tau = tau, weights = (1 - e) / e, method = "br"))
  q <- drop(G %*% fit$coefficients)
  r <- y - q
  (sum(r * (1 + Lambda^sign(r) * (1 - e) / e)) + sum(q / e)) / sum(1 / e)
}

qb_bounds <- function(y, X, e, Lambda) {
  c(lower = -qb_upper(-y, X, e, Lambda), upper = qb_upper(y, X, e, Lambda))
}

# ATE bounds: treated arm with e, control arm with 1 - e, then subtract.
msm_ate_bounds <- function(y, z, X, e, Lambda, method = c("qb", "zsb")) {
  method <- match.arg(method)
  arm <- function(idx, p) {
    if (method == "qb") return(qb_bounds(y[idx], X[idx, , drop = FALSE], p, Lambda))
    b <- msm_weight_box(p, Lambda)
    zsb_bounds(y[idx], b$lo, b$hi)
  }
  t1 <- arm(z == 1, e[z == 1])
  t0 <- arm(z == 0, 1 - e[z == 0])
  c(lower = unname(t1["lower"] - t0["upper"]),
    upper = unname(t1["upper"] - t0["lower"]))
}
```

At $\Lambda = 1$ both bounds equal the IPW estimate, 3.339 kg, with a
bootstrap interval from 2.354 to 4.320 kg. The bounds widen quickly. At
$\Lambda = 1.5$ the sharp bounds run from 1.035 to 5.725 kg and the lower end
of the interval is 0.178 kg. At $\Lambda = 1.6$ the lower end is $-0.157$ kg.
So a confounder that moved each person's odds of quitting by up to a factor of
1.5, beyond what the nine covariates predict, could not explain away the
weight gain; one that moved them by a factor of 1.6 could. The sharp point
bounds stay above zero up to $\Lambda = 1.8$, the ZSB bounds only up to 1.7.
The sharp bounds are narrower at every $\Lambda$; at $\Lambda = 2$ they are
7.961 kg wide against 8.672 kg.

How does $\Lambda$ relate to Rosenbaum's $\Gamma$? Each model contains the
other at a different value of its parameter
[@zhao-small-bhattacharya-2019, Proposition 7.1]. Suppose
@eq-sensitivity-rosenbaum holds with $\Gamma$. The nominal $e(x)$ is the
average of $e_0(x, U)$ over $U$, so its odds lie between the smallest and
largest true odds at $x$. Every true odds is then within a factor $\Gamma$ of
the odds of $e(x)$, and @eq-sensitivity-msm holds with $\Lambda = \Gamma$. In
the other direction, two units may sit a factor $\Lambda$ above and below the
odds of $e(x)$, so @eq-sensitivity-msm with $\Lambda$ gives
@eq-sensitivity-rosenbaum with $\Gamma = \Lambda^2$. A conclusion that
survives $\Lambda = 1.5$ therefore survives every confounder Rosenbaum's model
allows at $\Gamma = 1.5$. The two analyses above are still not directly
comparable. The matched analysis tests a sharp null for the ATT with
randomization inference; the weighting analysis bounds the ATE and uses the
bootstrap.

The four analyses do not share a scale. $\Gamma$ and $\Lambda$ bound odds
ratios of treatment, the E-value bounds a risk ratio, and the robustness value
is a partial $R^2$. They also ask different questions: $\Gamma$, $\Lambda$ and
the E-value ask when the result stops being significant, the robustness value
asks when the point estimate reaches zero. Reporting one of them is normal
practice; the numbers should not be averaged or compared directly.

## What about partial identification?

The bounding tradition here starts with Manski [-@manski-1990-bounds]; Lee [-@lee-2009-bounds] gives the treatment-selection bounds used in applied work.

When sensitivity analysis shows the point estimate is fragile, partial
identification is often the next step. Instead of one estimate, report bounds
under weaker assumptions.

The Manski (1990) "no-assumption" bounds say:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{ATE} &\ge \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}1]P(D{=}1) - \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}0]P(D{=}0) - K\,P(D{=}1), \\
\text{ATE} &\le \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}1]P(D{=}1) - \mathbb{E}[Y|D{=}0]P(D{=}0) + K\,P(D{=}0).
\end{aligned}
$$ {#eq-sensitivity-analysis-1}

Each potential-outcome mean is split by the law of total expectation, filling
in the unobserved arm with its worst/best case. With outcomes bounded in
$Y \in [0, K]$, this is a single interval of width exactly $K$ — bounded, but
often too wide to be useful. Tighter bounds come from adding structure to a
specific problem: Lee (2009) bounds address sample selection/attrition (the
outcome is observed only for selected units) by combining random assignment
with monotone selection and trimming the over-selected arm. For IV settings,
@mourifie-wan-2017 provide a test of the LATE identifying assumptions
(instrument validity plus monotonicity).

These bounds usually complement a point estimate. They are useful when the
point estimate depends too much on an untestable assumption. The marginal
sensitivity model sits between the two extremes: at $\Lambda = 1$ it gives the
point estimate, and as $\Lambda$ grows its sharp bounds widen toward the
no-assumption interval.

## Summary

- Sensitivity analysis asks how strong hidden confounding must be to change
  the result.
- Cinelli-Hazlett bounds are useful for regression estimates.
- E-values are useful for risk ratios.
- Rosenbaum bounds are useful for matched pairs.
- The marginal sensitivity model is useful for weighting estimators; Dorn and
  Guo's quantile-balancing bounds are sharp and need one quantile regression
  per arm.
- Observational causal estimates should usually include at least one
  sensitivity analysis.
